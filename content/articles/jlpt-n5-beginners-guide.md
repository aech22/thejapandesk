# JLPT N5合格を目指す学習ロードマップ

JLPT N5 is the entry-level tier of the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test, built for learners who have completed roughly the first hundred hours of basic instruction. Study vocabulary and kana reading first, move to core grammar patterns next, and only then layer in short reading passages and listening practice. Treat grammar and vocabulary as the foundation; reading and listening are where you apply what you've already memorized, not where you build it.

## What Level Does N5 Actually Test

N5 sits at the bottom of a five-level scale (N5 to N1, with N1 the most advanced). It checks whether you can understand basic Japanese used in everyday situations and in some classroom contexts. That means simple sentences about daily life, short set phrases, and straightforward exchanges rather than nuanced opinion or business language.

The test itself is split into sections covering vocabulary, grammar paired with reading, and listening. There is no speaking or writing component, which surprises some learners expecting a conversation test. You are never asked to produce sentences yourself; you are asked to recognize and choose the correct option among several.

Kanji at this level is limited to a small set of common characters, often cited as around 100, alongside hiragana and katakana, which you are expected to read fluently without romaji support. If you still rely on romanized Japanese to get through a sentence, that is the first gap to close before anything else.

## Why Order Matters Before You Start

A common mistake is starting with reading practice because it feels like "real" Japanese. The problem is that reading passages at N5 level assume you already know the grammar patterns and vocabulary used inside them. Without that foundation, you end up looking up nearly every word, which turns reading practice into vocabulary lookup practice, an inefficient use of study time.

The more reliable order is vocabulary and kana first, then grammar patterns, then reading and listening as integration exercises. This mirrors how the test itself is structured: grammar knowledge is tested partly through fill-in-the-blank sentences, and reading comprehension depends entirely on recognizing the words and structures already covered in the grammar section.

## Step One: Lock Down Kana and Core Vocabulary

Before opening any grammar textbook, make sure hiragana and katakana are automatic. You should be able to read a sentence in kana without mentally sounding out each character one by one. This usually takes a few weeks of daily practice with flashcards or a dedicated kana app, and skipping it creates friction at every later stage.

Once kana reading is comfortable, start building a core vocabulary list. N5 vocabulary lists are widely available and cover everyday nouns, common verbs, basic adjectives, and time or place words. Learning vocabulary in context, inside short example sentences, tends to stick better than memorizing isolated word lists, so look for materials that pair each word with a simple sentence.

## Step Two: Work Through Grammar Patterns Systematically

N5 grammar covers a manageable set of patterns: basic sentence structure, particles such as は, が, を, and に, verb conjugation in present and past tense, simple adjective conjugation, and basic connecting words. Most N5 textbooks organize these into fifteen to twenty short lessons, each introducing two or three new patterns with practice sentences.

Go through these in order rather than skipping to whatever seems interesting. Particle usage in particular builds cumulatively; later lessons assume you already understand how は and が behave differently in a sentence, and trying to shortcut this stage creates confusion that resurfaces during the actual grammar and reading section of the exam.

As you finish each grammar point, write a handful of your own example sentences using vocabulary from Step One. This forces the two skill sets to work together early, instead of treating grammar and vocabulary as separate tracks that only meet for the first time during a practice test.

## Step Three: Add Reading and Listening Practice

Once a solid share of N5 grammar and vocabulary feels familiar, start short reading passages designed for this level. These are usually only a few sentences long and ask simple comprehension questions: who did what, when, or where. If a passage feels impossible, that is a signal to review the underlying grammar point rather than to push through more reading blind.

Listening practice should start around the same time. N5 listening sections use slow, clearly enunciated speech describing everyday situations, such as asking for directions or ordering food. Short audio clips paired with comprehension questions work well here, and repeating the same clip a few times before checking the transcript builds the habit of catching meaning from sound rather than mentally translating word by word.

## Building a Weekly Study Rhythm

A workable approach for most self-study learners is to rotate through all four skills each week rather than finishing one completely before touching the next. Spend the early part of a study block on new vocabulary and grammar, then dedicate shorter sessions later in the week to reading and listening that reuse what was just learned. This keeps the four skills connected instead of siloed.

Practice tests become useful only after the first full pass through grammar and vocabulary material, typically a few weeks to a couple of months in depending on study pace. Taking a full practice test too early mostly measures what you haven't learned yet, which is discouraging and not very informative. Save timed practice for closer to the actual exam date, when it can reveal pacing problems and weak spots worth a final review.

## What to Check Before Exam Day

Confirm the exact test date, application deadline, and test center location through the official JLPT administering body for your country, since these details vary by region and change from year to year. Also check the current list of required materials to bring, as photo identification and admission ticket rules are enforced strictly and vary slightly by test site.

Finally, make sure your study materials match the current N5 grammar and vocabulary scope, since published word lists occasionally get revised. A textbook or app explicitly labeled for N5, updated within the last few years, is a safer bet than older material that may reference a slightly different syllabus.
