# Mount Fuji Hike: Trails, Huts and Pacing for First-Timers

If you're climbing Mount Fuji for the first time, pick the Yoshida Trail unless you have a specific reason not to: it has the most huts, the clearest crowds for safety, and the easiest access from Tokyo. Climb slowly, book a hut around the seventh or eighth station to break the ascent in two, and treat any headache above the fifth station as a signal to slow down rather than push through. The rest of this guide explains why.

## Which trail should you choose

Four trails lead to the summit, and they start from different sides of the mountain. The Yoshida Trail, starting from the Yamanashi side, is the busiest and the best equipped. It has the highest concentration of mountain huts, first-aid stations, and vending machines, which matters more than it sounds like it should when you're exhausted at 3am. Because it's also the most crowded, expect a slow, shuffling queue near the top during peak summer weekends.

The Fujinomiya Trail, on the Shizuoka side, is shorter in distance but steeper, with a rockier path. It suits hikers who want a quicker ascent and don't mind less hut spacing on the way up. The Subashiri Trail joins the Yoshida route partway up, so it offers a quieter lower section before merging into the same crowds higher up. The Gotemba Trail is the longest and least developed, with huts spaced far apart and a long stretch of volcanic ash underfoot; it's generally better suited to experienced hikers who want solitude and don't need frequent rest points.

For a first attempt, the trade-off usually comes down to this: Yoshida gives you support and company but also lines; Fujinomiya gives you speed but less cushioning if something goes wrong; Gotemba gives you space but demands more self-sufficiency. Subashiri sits in between.

## How the climb is structured, station by station

All four trails are divided into numbered stations, and most hikers start partway up, at the fifth station, which each trail reaches by road or bus. From there, the climb to the summit typically takes most hikers somewhere in the range of five to seven hours, though pace varies enormously with fitness, group size, and how much you stop.

The stations above the fifth are where the huts sit, and they are not evenly spaced. Lower stations tend to be closer together; the gaps often widen as you climb, so the second half of the night can feel longer than the first even though the map suggests otherwise. This is one reason guided groups and experienced climbers tend to treat the seventh and eighth stations as natural waypoints rather than trying to judge progress purely by distance.

## How to use the mountain huts

Mountain huts on Fuji serve two purposes: a place to rest and warm up, and a place to sleep for a few hours before a pre-dawn summit push. Few hikers do the whole climb in one unbroken push from the fifth station; most book a hut partway up, arrive in the evening, eat something, sleep in a shared bunk-style room for a short stretch, then set out again around midnight or in the early hours to reach the summit for sunrise.

Book your hut before the climb, not on the day. Huts fill up fast in peak season, and arriving without a reservation means you may find nothing available at the altitude where you need it most. When you check in, expect a simple process: you'll be shown your sleeping space, told the mealtimes if meals are included, and given a rough idea of when other guests in your area plan to wake up, since most huts run on a shared schedule timed to the standard sunrise push.

Use the huts for more than sleep. Even hikers who aren't staying overnight stop at huts along the way to sit down, refill water, use the toilet, and warm up with a hot drink. Treat every hut you pass as a checkpoint: a few minutes off your feet every hour or so does more for your pace over the full climb than pushing straight through and then needing a longer stop later.

## How to pace yourself

The single biggest mistake first-time climbers make is walking too fast on the lower slopes because the trail feels easy and the air still feels normal. Altitude thins out gradually, but its effects compound, and the pace that feels comfortable at the fifth station is often too fast for the ninth.

Walk slower than feels necessary for the first hour. Keep your steps short, especially on loose volcanic gravel, since short steps use less energy per stride and reduce slipping. Drink water regularly rather than waiting until you're thirsty, and eat small amounts often rather than one large meal, since a full stomach at altitude can make nausea worse.

Build in a deliberate acclimatization stop. Many guides and experienced climbers recommend resting at the fifth station for thirty minutes to an hour before starting, simply sitting and letting your body adjust to the elevation change from the drive or bus up. It feels like wasted time when you're eager to start, but it reduces the odds of altitude symptoms showing up higher on the mountain, when turning back is more costly.

## What altitude sickness looks like and what to do about it

Headache, nausea, dizziness, and unusual fatigue are the early signs, and they can appear anywhere above the fifth station, not just near the summit. These symptoms are common enough on Fuji that huts are used to dealing with them, and most sell bottled oxygen as a stopgap measure.

The only real treatment is descending or at least stopping your ascent until symptoms ease. Pushing on through a worsening headache is how minor altitude sickness turns into a trip-ending problem. If you or someone in your group starts feeling notably worse rather than just tired, stop at the nearest hut, rest, and reassess before continuing. There is no summit view worth risking a serious altitude reaction for.

## What to check before you go

Trail opening dates change year to year and are set separately for each of the four routes, so confirm current dates and any trail-specific advisories before booking transport or huts. Weather on Fuji changes fast regardless of the forecast at the base, and hikers who started in warm, clear conditions at the fifth station can hit wind, cold rain, or fog within a couple of hours of climbing. Pack layers, rain gear, a headlamp, and more water than you think you'll need, and confirm your hut's check-in and wake-up routine directly with them when you book, since arrangements vary hut to hut.
