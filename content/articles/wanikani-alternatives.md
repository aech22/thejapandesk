## 1. The pain point: WaniKani is good, but it isn't for everyone — and you can feel it

Let me guess why you're here.

You either tried WaniKani and something rubbed you the wrong way, or you're about to pay for it and a quiet voice is asking *is this really the only option?* Both are healthy instincts.

WaniKani is genuinely excellent at one thing: it teaches you to *read* around 2,000 kanji and 6,000 vocabulary words through radicals, mnemonic stories, and a strict spaced-repetition system. When it clicks, it really clicks. But it has real, well-known frustrations, and they're the reason "WaniKani alternatives" is such a common search:

- **It's deliberately slow.** You cannot rush ahead. The SRS gates each level, and even a strong learner takes well over a year to finish. That pacing is by design, and for some people it's maddening.
- **You can't skip what you already know.** Come in with 300 kanji already? Doesn't matter. You start at the bottom and grind through radicals you've known for years.
- **It teaches zero grammar and no listening.** It's a reading tool, period. No sentences you produce, no audio comprehension, no speaking.
- **It's not aligned to the JLPT.** If your goal is passing a specific JLPT level by a deadline, WaniKani's ordering doesn't map to that.
- **The mnemonics don't work for everyone.** Some brains love the goofy stories. Others find them more work to remember than the kanji itself.
- **The price adds up.** Monthly, yearly, or a lifetime fee — and if you stall out at level 20, you paid for 60.

None of that makes WaniKani bad. It makes it *specific*. If its specific shape doesn't fit your brain, your budget, or your goal, there are strong alternatives — some free, some radically different in philosophy. Let's find yours.

## 3. My three criteria for a kanji tool

**Criterion 1 — Retention over cramming.** Kanji is a long game. A good tool has to use spaced repetition (or something as effective) so that what you learn in March is still there in September. Any tool that just shows you characters once fails this immediately.

**Criterion 2 — Control and fit.** Can you go at your own pace? Skip what you know? Adjust the order to your goal (JLPT, reading, a specific textbook)? WaniKani's biggest complaints are all about *control*, so I weigh flexibility heavily.

**Criterion 3 — Does it connect kanji to real Japanese?** Kanji learned as isolated characters is brittle. The tools I rate highest tie characters to vocabulary, and ideally to real sentences, so the knowledge is *usable*, not trivia. As a native speaker I'll be blunt: nobody reads kanji one character at a time. You read words. A tool that treats kanji as words-in-context is teaching you the real skill.

## 5. The reviews — pros and cons of each

### Anki — the free, infinitely flexible workhorse

**What it is:** An open-source spaced-repetition flashcard engine. Not Japanese-specific — it's the SRS that half the medical students and language learners on earth quietly rely on.

**Pros:** Free on desktop, Android, and the web. Total control — pace, order, card design, everything. Enormous library of shared Japanese decks (the "Kaishi 1.5k" and various core vocab decks are popular starting points). Because you can put *anything* on a card, it grows with you from kanji to full sentence mining.

**Cons:** The out-of-box experience is spartan and a little intimidating. You either curate a shared deck or build your own, and bad card design leads to bad results. The iOS app is a one-time paid purchase (this funds the whole free project, so it's fair, but it surprises people). No mnemonics, no hand-holding, no "next level" dopamine.

**Native-speaker note:** Anki is only as good as the deck. Use a deck that teaches kanji *inside vocabulary and sentences*, not isolated characters with a single English keyword. "生" attached to one English word will actively mislead you — that character shifts meaning and reading wildly across words (生きる, 生徒, 芝生). A sentence-based deck fixes that. In my experience, this single choice — good deck vs. bad deck — matters more than which app you pick.

**Verdict:** If you like control and don't mind a learning curve, Anki is the highest-ceiling option here, and it's free. It's also the tool you'll probably still be using years from now.

### Renshuu — the best free all-rounder, and my default recommendation

**What it is:** A free, community-driven platform covering vocabulary, kanji, *and* grammar with its own SRS, wrapped in a friendly, almost game-like interface.

**Pros:** Genuinely generous free tier — this isn't a crippled demo. Covers more than kanji, so it can be closer to a complete study home. Good pacing control. Active, kind community. It quietly satisfies all three of my criteria at once.

**Cons:** The interface is charming but busy, and the sheer number of features can overwhelm a brand-new learner. Less "famous" than WaniKani, so fewer polished third-party guides. The optional premium's value depends on how deep you go.

**Native-speaker note:** I like that Renshuu keeps kanji connected to vocabulary and grammar in the same place, because that's how the language actually lives — characters, words, and particles aren't separate subjects, they're one system. Learning them together is more faithful to real Japanese than siloing kanji off by itself.

**Verdict:** For most people asking "what's a good WaniKani alternative," this is my first answer, largely because it's free and it does more.

### Bunpro — if the JLPT is your finish line

**What it is:** Primarily a *grammar* SRS — spaced repetition applied to every particle and conjugation from N5 to N1 — that also folds in vocabulary and kanji study.

**Pros:** Best-in-class for grammar, and its ordering follows the JLPT ladder, which WaniKani doesn't. If your goal is a dated exam, that alignment is a real advantage. Free tier plus a long no-card-required trial, and a modest lifetime option.

**Cons:** Its kanji offering is secondary to its grammar mission — it's not a pure WaniKani replacement so much as a different pillar that happens to touch kanji. If kanji is *all* you want, this is not the tightest fit.

**Verdict:** Best thought of as a *complement*. Many strong self-studiers run a kanji tool (Anki or Renshuu) alongside Bunpro for grammar. If you're JLPT-driven, it belongs in your stack.

### Migaku — learn kanji from the content you already love

**What it is:** A completely different philosophy. Instead of a fixed deck, Migaku turns real content — Netflix, YouTube, articles — into study material. You click unknown words, get instant definitions and audio, and one-click them into flashcards mined from actual sentences.

**Pros:** Kanji and vocabulary arrive *in context*, from content you actually want to watch — which is exactly the "connect it to real Japanese" ideal in my third criterion. Highly motivating because the material is genuinely interesting to you. It's also multi-skill (reading, listening) rather than kanji-only.

**Cons:** Best for upper-beginners and up — you need enough foundation for immersion to pay off, so a total beginner may flounder. It's a paid subscription, not free. There's a real learning curve to the sentence-mining workflow.

**Verdict:** If your dream is to understand anime and native media (and honestly, whose isn't), Migaku attacks kanji from that direction. I've written a full Migaku-vs-alternatives comparison for exactly this decision.

### KaniWani / reverse-WaniKani tools — for production, not just recognition

**What it is:** Free companion tools that flip the direction — instead of "see kanji, recall meaning," you get "see English, produce the Japanese word." Some sync to your WaniKani progress.

**Pros:** Fills a real gap. Recognizing a word when you read it is easier than *producing* it when you want to speak or write. Reverse drilling builds active recall. Free.

**Cons:** Narrow by design, and most useful alongside WaniKani rather than instead of it. Not a standalone kanji curriculum.

**Verdict:** A useful *add-on*, not a replacement — but if your complaint is "I recognize kanji but can't recall words to use them," this is the targeted fix.

### Skritter — if you actually want to write kanji by hand

**What it is:** An SRS focused on *handwriting* — you trace and write characters stroke by stroke on screen.

**Pros:** The only tool here that trains the motor skill of writing. Great for learners who want to send handwritten notes, take a class, or simply feel that writing cements the character.

**Cons:** Subscription-based. Handwriting is, frankly, a lower-priority skill for most modern learners who mostly type — and typing Japanese only needs *recognition*, not production strokes. Niche unless writing is a personal goal.

**Verdict:** Specialist tool. Wonderful if handwriting matters to you, skippable if it doesn't. Be honest about whether you'll ever handwrite Japanese.

## 6. Recommendation by use case

**"I just want the best free WaniKani alternative":** Renshuu. Free, structured, covers kanji + vocab + grammar. Start there.

**"I want maximum control and I'll happily tinker":** Anki with a good sentence-based deck. Highest ceiling, zero cost on most platforms, yours forever.

**"I'm studying for a specific JLPT level by a deadline":** Bunpro for grammar + Renshuu or Anki for kanji. The JLPT ordering matters here.

**"I want to learn kanji from anime and shows I actually watch":** Migaku. Different philosophy, immersion-first — see my dedicated comparison. (Pair it with a listening course like JapanesePod101 to build the ear underneath it.)

**"I can recognize kanji but can't recall words to speak":** Add KaniWani-style reverse drilling to whatever you use.

**"I want to write by hand":** Skritter, as a specialist add-on.

**And if you're still tempted by WaniKani itself?** That's fine. If the mnemonic-and-radical approach fits your brain and the slow pace doesn't bother you, it's a polished, effective tool. The point of this article isn't that WaniKani is bad — it's that it's *specific*, and now you know the alternatives if its shape doesn't fit yours.
