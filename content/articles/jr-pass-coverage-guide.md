# What the Japan Rail Pass Actually Covers: Trains, Lines and Limits

The nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS covers almost every train operated by the six JR companies, including most shinkansen services, JR limited express and rapid and local trains, some JR bus routes, and the JR-West ferry to Miyajima. It does not cover the fastest Tokaido/Sanyo shinkansen services (Nozomi and Mizuho), and it does not cover any private railway, subway, or municipal tram, even when that line runs parallel to a JR route or shares a station building with one. Knowing which category a given journey falls into is the whole trick to using the pass well.

## Shinkansen: covered, with one big exception

The pass works on almost all shinkansen lines: Tohoku, Joetsu, Hokuriku, Hokkaido, Sanyo, Kyushu, and the rest. The exception that trips up first-time visitors is the Tokaido-Sanyo corridor between Tokyo and Hakata. On that line, the fastest trains — Nozomi and Mizuho — are excluded entirely. Pass holders ride the slightly slower Hikari and Sakura services instead, which stop more often but still cover the same route in a reasonable time. For someone going from Tokyo to Kyoto or Osaka, this means boarding a Hikari rather than a Nozomi, not a different kind of ticket altogether — the seat is still a reserved shinkansen seat, just on a different train.

## Limited express, rapid, and local JR trains

Away from the shinkansen, the pass covers the full range of JR East, JR West, JR Central, JR Hokkaido, JR Shikoku, and JR Kyushu trains, from limited express services like the Narita Express or the Odoriko down through rapid and ordinary local trains. This is where the pass earns its keep on multi-stop itineraries: a loop through Tokyo, Nagano, Kanazawa, Kyoto, and Hiroshima runs almost entirely on JR tracks, so the pass handles nearly the whole trip without separate tickets. Seat reservations on these trains are included in the Ordinary pass and the Green pass alike, so pass holders can reserve seats at JR ticket offices or machines at no extra charge, subject to availability.

## JR buses and the Miyajima ferry

A smaller but useful category: some JR-operated bus routes are covered, particularly where they substitute for a rail line in rural areas, such as parts of the JR Bus network in Kyushu or around Nikko. The JR-West ferry connecting Miyajimaguchi Station to Miyajima is also covered, which matters for anyone heading to the floating torii gate near Hiroshima — the short ferry ride is treated as part of the rail journey rather than a separate paid crossing.

## What is excluded: private railways and subways

This is the category that causes the most confusion, because private and JR lines often sit in the same station or run along the same corridor. The pass does not cover Tokyo Metro or Toei Subway, Keikyu, Keisei, Odakyu, Tobu, Seibu, Hankyu, Hanshin, Keihan, Kintetsu, Nankai, or any other private railway. It also does not cover municipal trams or monorails that aren't JR-operated, including the Yurikamome in Tokyo or the Tokyo Monorail to Haneda in most cases. If a route to a particular shrine or neighborhood requires one of these lines, that fare is paid separately, in cash or with an IC card, regardless of whether a JR pass is in hand.

This matters most around Tokyo and Osaka, where sightseeing routes frequently cross from JR tracks onto a private line for the last stretch — reaching Nikko's Toshogu area beyond the JR station, for instance, or getting to several Kyoto temples served only by private rail or bus. The fix isn't to assume JR coverage; it's to check the specific line for each leg before boarding.

## Regional and area passes versus the nationwide pass

Alongside the nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS, each JR company sells its own regional passes with narrower geographic coverage, and these follow the same JR-only logic. The JR EAST PASS, the JR TOKYO Wide Pass, the Hokuriku Arch Pass, the JR Tohoku-South Hokkaido Rail Pass, and the various Kansai-area passes from JR West all cover JR lines within their stated region and exclude private railways and subways in the same way the nationwide pass does. Someone planning a trip confined to Tokyo and nearby day trips, or to Kansai and western Honshu, may find one of these regional passes fits the itinerary better than the nationwide version, simply because the travel stays inside JR territory the whole time.

For context on what the nationwide pass itself costs: for exchange orders purchased through 30 September 2026, the Ordinary nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS is ¥50,000 for seven days, ¥80,000 for fourteen days, and ¥100,000 for twenty-one days for adults, with the Green pass at ¥70,000, ¥110,000, and ¥140,000. From 1 October 2026, those figures rise to ¥53,000, ¥84,000, and ¥105,000 for the Ordinary pass and ¥74,000, ¥116,000, and ¥147,000 for Green. Whichever duration suits the trip, the coverage rules described above apply the same way regardless of price tier — Green just buys access to Green Car carriages on covered trains, not a wider set of lines.

## Working out whether the pass fits a specific trip

The practical test is to lay out the itinerary leg by leg and ask, for each one, whether JR operates the train. A trip built around shinkansen hops between major cities, with local JR trains filling in the gaps, is the classic case where the pass pays for itself through sheer frequency of use. A trip that's mostly subway rides within Tokyo, or mostly private-railway trips to specific temples and shrines, gets far less benefit from the nationwide pass, since so much of the travel sits outside JR's network.

It also helps to check, before departure, whether the destination station is actually a JR station or just shares a name with one. Several cities have a JR station and a private-railway station a short walk apart, both carrying similar names, and only one of them accepts the pass.

## What to check locally before relying on the pass

Seat reservation rules can change by train and by season, and the pass does not override train-specific reservation requirements during peak periods. It's worth confirming at a JR ticket office or through the operator's own reservation system that a particular shinkansen or limited express service is covered before counting on it, especially for Nozomi-adjacent routes where it's easy to board the wrong train by habit. For any leg involving a private railway, subway, or non-JR bus, budget for a separate fare, and keep an IC card loaded for exactly those gaps — it's the simplest way to move seamlessly between JR and non-JR legs without stopping to buy a paper ticket each time.
