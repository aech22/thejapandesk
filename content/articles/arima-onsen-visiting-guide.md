# Arima Onsen Etiquette: Gold and Silver Bath Manners Explained

Arima Onsen has two distinct waters, kinsen (gold spring) and ginsen (silver spring), and most visitors try both in a single visit. There is no official rule on which to bathe in first, but many first-time visitors start with ginsen, the lighter, more neutral water, and finish with kinsen, the iron-rich water that leaves skin feeling warm for longer. Beyond that sequencing, the manners are the same as any Japanese onsen: wash before soaking, no swimsuits, hair tied up, and no towel in the water. The difference at Arima is less about rules and more about understanding what the two waters actually are.

## What kinsen and ginsen actually mean

Kinsen, the gold spring, gets its reddish-brown color from dissolved iron and salt. It oxidizes on contact with air, which is why the water looks clear when it first comes out of the ground and turns darker in the bath itself. It is known locally for being good for the skin and for staying warm in the body after bathing, though no guide should promise specific health effects.

Ginsen, the silver spring, is colorless and contains dissolved carbon dioxide and radium. It feels lighter on the skin and is often described as more refreshing than kinsen. Some facilities carbonate it further, so bubbles cling to the skin while soaking.

Not every ryokan in town has both. Some properties draw only kinsen, some only ginsen, and a few pipe in both. The two public bathhouses in the center of town, Kin no Yu and Gin no Yu, are named for exactly this reason: one specializes in gold water, the other in silver. If a visitor wants to compare both waters in one day without booking a ryokan stay, these two public baths are the simplest way to do it, since they sit a short walk apart in the old town.

## The order and the basic steps

There is no fixed, town-wide protocol dictating that gold must come before silver. What matters more is the sequence everyone follows inside a single bath, regardless of which water it is:

Rinse at the washing stations before entering the tub. These stations have stools, handheld showers, and sometimes bodywash and shampoo. Sit, wash thoroughly, and rinse off soap completely before stepping into the shared water. Skipping this step is the single most common mistake visitors from outside Japan make, and it is the one that other bathers notice immediately.

Enter without a swimsuit. Japanese public baths are bathed in without clothing, and swimsuits are not permitted in the water itself, even at many baths marketed toward international visitors. A small modesty towel can be carried to the bath area but should not touch the water; most people fold it and set it on their head or beside the tub.

Tie back long hair so it does not fall into the water. Move slowly and keep voices low. Splashing, swimming, or diving are treated the same way they would be in a quiet library.

There is no need to shower again after soaking unless a visitor wants to rinse off the mineral residue, particularly from kinsen, which can leave a faint iron smell on skin and hair. Many ryokan leave a small basin of fresh water near the exit for exactly this purpose.

## Tattoos and other local realities

Tattoo policy varies by facility rather than by town-wide rule, and Arima is no exception. Some ryokan and public baths still restrict visible tattoos, often for reasons tied to local association with organized crime rather than any aesthetic objection, while others have relaxed the policy or offer private baths as a workaround. Anyone with tattoos should check the specific facility's current policy before arriving, since Arima's mix of very old ryokan and more recently renovated bathhouses means practices differ from one address to the next.

Skin sensitivity is another local detail worth knowing in advance. Kinsen's iron and salt content is stronger than the water found at many other hot spring towns, and some visitors notice their skin feels tight or slightly irritated after a long soak, especially on a first visit. Shorter sessions with breaks are common advice from staff at ryokan that offer kinsen, and it is reasonable to step out, cool down, and rehydrate rather than treating the bath as something to endure in one long sitting.

## How Arima differs from other onsen towns

Arima's structure is different from towns built around an outdoor bathhouse-hopping culture. In Kinosaki, visitors buy a pass and move between several public bathhouses scattered through town over the course of an evening, dressed in the ryokan's yukata, with the Kinosaki public baths priced at ¥800 per adult visit and ¥400 for children aged 3 to primary school, or an all-day Yumepa pass at ¥1,500 for adults and ¥750 for children. In Kurokawa, the custom is a stamp-card system: the Kurokawa nyuto-tegata costs ¥1,500 for adults and ¥700 for children from age 3 to sixth grade, comes with three stickers for three different outdoor baths, stays valid for six months, and can be used between 8:30 and 21:00.

Arima does not run on either model. There is no town-wide hopping pass, and the core experience centers on the two named public bathhouses, Kin no Yu and Gin no Yu, plus whichever water a visitor's own ryokan happens to pipe in. Day-trip bathing, known locally as higaeri nyuyoku, is common and lets someone staying in Osaka or Kobe visit a ryokan's bath for a few hours without an overnight stay, but this is arranged ryokan by ryokan rather than through a single shared ticket. A visitor used to Kinosaki's evening bathhouse circuit or Kurokawa's stamp card should expect something closer to choosing one or two specific baths for the day rather than moving freely between many.

## Putting this together before a visit

Someone staying overnight should check which water their ryokan offers, kinsen, ginsen, or both, and plan a short visit to whichever public bathhouse covers the other one. A day visitor arriving from Kobe or Osaka should decide in advance whether to try Kin no Yu, Gin no Yu, or a ryokan's day-use bath, since walking between the old town's narrow streets with a heavy bag is less convenient than at towns built around a single bathing loop.

Anyone with visible tattoos should confirm the policy directly with the specific ryokan or bathhouse rather than assuming a blanket rule applies across town. Visitors with sensitive skin should treat kinsen with some caution on a first try, keeping soaks shorter and taking breaks. And everyone, regardless of experience with Japanese baths elsewhere, should expect the same washing-first, swimsuit-free, quiet-voices etiquette that applies nationwide, layered on top of Arima's own choice between gold and silver water.
