# Learn Japanese Calligraphy (Shodo): What Beginners Should Know Before Starting

Start with four tools, not a full set: a brush (fude), ink (sumi), an inkstone (suzuri), and practice paper (hanshi). Learn the basic stroke order on simple kanji before touching cursive styles, and choose a class over self-study if you want correction on brush angle and pressure, which is hard to judge from video alone. Self-study works fine for building a daily habit once you already know the fundamentals.

## What tools does a beginner actually need

Traditional shodo uses a small set of tools known collectively as the "four treasures of the study" (bunbo shiho): the brush, ink, inkstone, and paper. A beginner does not need all the variations sold in art shops.

The brush (fude) matters most. A single medium-sized brush with a pointed tip handles most beginner exercises — large single characters, then smaller multi-character lines later. Natural animal-hair brushes hold ink and taper better than synthetic ones, but a reasonable synthetic brush is a sensible first purchase; it is more forgiving if it dries out between sessions and it is easier to clean.

Ink comes in two forms. A solid inkstick (sumi) is ground against the inkstone with water to make liquid ink, which is the traditional method and also a kind of warm-up ritual — grinding ink slowly before writing is supposed to settle the mind. Bottled liquid ink (bokuju) skips that step and is what most beginners and classrooms use day to day, saving it for occasional practice.

The inkstone (suzuri) is a flat stone slab with a shallow well. Even a basic stone or resin version works for beginners; the expensive natural stone pieces are for people who grind their own ink regularly.

For paper, practice sheets (hanshi) are thin, absorbent, and sold in packs meant to be used up quickly — shodo is practiced in volume, not preserved one sheet at a time. A felt mat (shitajiki) goes underneath to stop ink from bleeding through to the table, and a paperweight (bunchin) holds the paper flat while writing, since the brush moves without a steadying hand on the page the way a pen does.

A beginner set that bundles a brush, ink, inkstone, mat, and a weight is usually enough to start. There is no need to buy a full range of brush sizes or inkstick types before knowing which style of writing interests you.

## What order should basic brush strokes be learned in

Shodo is built from a fixed vocabulary of strokes, and most instruction — whether in a book, a video series, or a classroom — introduces them in roughly the same order.

The first lesson is usually posture and grip: sitting upright, holding the brush more vertically than a pen, and controlling pressure with the fingers and wrist rather than gripping tightly. This matters because the thickness and taper of a stroke come entirely from how hard the brush is pressed and how fast it moves, not from the brush itself.

After grip, students practice the basic strokes that make up most kanji: the horizontal stroke, the vertical stroke, the left-falling stroke (hidari-harai), the right-falling stroke (migi-harai), and the hook (hane). Each has a beginning, a middle, and an end that follows a visible rhythm — press down to start, pull through at a steady speed, then either lift cleanly or taper off, depending on the stroke.

Once individual strokes feel controlled, practice moves to simple characters that combine two or three of them, such as 一, 二, or 十, before progressing to more complex kanji with multiple stroke types in one character. Stroke order (the sequence in which the lines of a kanji are written) is taught alongside this, since writing strokes in the wrong order changes how the character balances on the page, even if the final shape looks similar.

Only after basic block-style writing (kaisho) feels stable do students usually move toward semi-cursive (gyosho) or cursive (sosho) styles, where strokes connect and the brush lifts less often. Trying cursive styles too early tends to produce shapes that look loose rather than fluid, because the underlying stroke control isn't there yet.

## Should a beginner learn shodo alone or in a class

Both paths work, but they solve different problems.

Self-study, using books or video lessons, is good for building the habit of regular practice and for learning the vocabulary of strokes and tool care at your own pace. It is well suited to someone who wants a quiet daily practice without committing to a schedule, or who is trying shodo briefly during a trip and wants to understand the basics beforehand.

A class, taught by an instructor in person, is better for correcting things a beginner cannot easily see in their own work: how much the wrist should rotate mid-stroke, whether the brush angle is drifting, and whether pressure is being released too early at the end of a stroke. These are small physical habits, and they are much easier to fix with someone watching and adjusting your hand than by comparing your paper to a printed example afterward. Many community centers, cultural centers, and some temples offer short beginner sessions aimed at visitors, which is a reasonable way to get hands-on correction without a long-term commitment.

A practical middle path is to use a class or a one-off workshop to learn grip, posture, and the basic strokes correctly, then continue practicing independently at home with hanshi paper and liquid ink, returning to a class periodically for feedback as you progress to more complex characters or cursive styles.

## What beginners should check before starting

Check what a workshop or class actually provides before attending. Some beginner sessions supply all the tools and simply charge for the experience; others expect participants to bring their own brush and ink. If you are short on time during a trip, a single drop-in session with tools provided is the simplest way to try shodo without buying equipment you may not use again.

If you plan to continue at home, keep expectations modest for the first few weeks. Early practice sheets will look uneven, and that unevenness is normal — it comes from inconsistent pressure and speed, which only steadies with repetition. Clean the brush thoroughly after each session and let it dry hanging tip-down; ink left to harden in the bristles shortens a brush's life quickly and is the most common beginner mistake with equipment care.

Finally, treat stroke order and basic kanji practice as the foundation rather than a formality. Students who skip ahead to decorative or cursive writing before the basic strokes are solid often find themselves relearning grip and pressure control later, which takes longer than building it correctly from the start.
