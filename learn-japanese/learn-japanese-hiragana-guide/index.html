How to Learn Japanese Hiragana: A Self-Study Guide

Start with the five vowel sounds, then work through hiragana in the standard gojuon order, spending about a week on recognition before you touch stroke order drills seriously. Once you can read all 46 base characters without hesitating, move to katakana using the same method, and only then bring in your first kanji alongside vocabulary you’re already using. Trying to learn all three writing systems at once is the most common reason people quit.

Why hiragana comes first

Hiragana is the foundation of written Japanese. It spells every native Japanese word that doesn’t have a kanji assigned to it, and it’s used for grammar particles, verb endings, and furigana readings printed above kanji in textbooks and manga aimed at children. If you can read hiragana fluently, you can already sound out a meaningful portion of any Japanese sentence, even before you know what the words mean.

It’s also the smallest of the three writing systems in terms of memorization load. Forty-six base characters, compared to another forty-six for katakana and thousands for kanji. Get hiragana solid first and everything after it moves faster, because your brain already has a working system for how Japanese syllables are built and combined.

The order that actually works

Most textbooks and apps teach hiragana in gojuon order, which groups characters by consonant sound: あ (a), い (i), う (u), え (e), お (o), then か (ka), き (ki), く (ku), け (ke), こ (ko), and so on through さ, た, な, は, ま, や, ら, わ, and ん.

There’s a reason this order has survived for centuries: it’s not arbitrary. Once you know the five vowel sounds, every other row is built from the same five vowels with a different consonant in front. Learning あいうえお first means you’re not memorizing 46 unrelated shapes — you’re memorizing 9 consonant patterns applied to a pattern you already know.

A practical week-by-week breakdown:

  • Days 1-2: あ row and か row (10 characters)
  • Days 3-4: さ row and た row (10 characters)
  • Days 5-6: な row, は row, ま row (15 characters)
  • Day 7: や, ら, わ, ん, and review of everything (11 characters)

After that first week, spend a second week doing nothing but review and reading practice. Recognition speed matters more than perfect handwriting at this stage.

Practice methods that make characters stick

Writing each character by hand, even if you’ll mostly type Japanese later, builds a muscle memory link between the shape and the sound that flashcards alone don’t create. A cheap notebook with grid squares works fine; you don’t need special stationery.

Mnemonics help with the characters that don’t click on their own. め (me) looks like an eye if you squint, and そ (so) is often remembered as a sewing needle pulling thread. You don’t need to invent these yourself — plenty of hiragana charts pair each character with a small illustration for exactly this reason. Use whatever sticks, even if the association feels silly.

Spaced repetition apps are useful for the recognition side: seeing a character and recalling its sound quickly, without translating through English first. The habit you’re building is direct sound recognition, not decoding. If you find yourself mentally saying “that’s the one that looks like a fishhook, which is the ka sound,” you’re still one step removed from fluency — the goal is for か to simply sound like “ka” in your head, the way the letter B doesn’t make you think of anything before you say its sound.

Reading practice matters as much as writing practice. Children’s books, NHK’s easy-news site, and hiragana-only practice sentences in beginner textbooks all give you real reading reps. Reading a sentence slowly and sounding it out, even without understanding every word, reinforces the character-to-sound mapping far more than isolated flashcards do.

When you’re ready to move to katakana

A reasonable benchmark: if you can read a random hiragana word without pausing on more than one or two characters, you’re ready to start katakana. Don’t wait for perfection — katakana uses the same gojuon order and the same underlying logic, so starting it doesn’t compete with hiragana review; it reinforces the pattern you’ve already built.

Katakana is used for foreign loanwords, some onomatopoeia, and emphasis, similar to italics in English. Because so many katakana words are borrowed from English — コーヒー (coffee), テレビ (television) — you’ll often recognize the meaning instantly even while you’re still slow at reading the shapes. That built-in feedback loop makes katakana faster to learn than hiragana for most people, even though the characters themselves are less visually intuitive.

Run both systems in parallel for a couple of weeks: hiragana review in the morning, katakana practice in the evening, so neither goes stale while you build the second one.

Moving into kanji without losing your hiragana base

Once katakana feels comfortable, kanji is the natural next step, but the transition works best when it’s gradual and tied to vocabulary rather than treated as a separate memorization project. Learning the kanji for a word you already know in hiragana — for example switching from writing a common verb entirely in hiragana to writing its stem in kanji — anchors the new character to something you can already read and say.

Many beginner textbooks introduce kanji with furigana, small hiragana characters printed above or beside the kanji showing its reading. This is exactly why a solid hiragana foundation pays off later: furigana only helps if you can read it instantly, without it becoming its own decoding puzzle.

Start with kanji that appear constantly — numbers, days of the week, common verbs like to go and to eat — rather than working through a frequency list mechanically. Recognizing kanji in context, in sentences you’re already reading, sticks better than isolated kanji drilling.

Common mistakes to avoid

Trying to master perfect stroke order before you can read fluently slows most self-learners down. Stroke order matters for handwriting speed and for guessing unfamiliar characters later, but recognition speed matters more in the first month.

Skipping reading practice in favor of only writing drills is another common gap. You can often write a character from memory faster than you can recognize it instantly in running text, and reading speed is what you’ll actually need.

Finally, resist the urge to learn hiragana, katakana, and kanji all at the same pace from day one. The sequence — hiragana, then katakana, then kanji layered on top of vocabulary — exists because each stage makes the next one easier, not because it’s the only order that technically works.
