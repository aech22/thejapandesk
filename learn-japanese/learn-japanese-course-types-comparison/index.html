Learn Japanese Course: Self-Study vs Online vs School

If you need structure and accountability, pick an online course with live instructors or a classroom program. If you are disciplined and mainly want to read, pick self-study materials paired with an app for speaking practice. If you need a visa, academic credit, or a fast path to fluency for work or study, a full-time language school or university program is the only option that delivers that pace. Most learners actually end up mixing two of these rather than picking one and sticking with it forever.

What separates the four main paths

Self-study means textbooks, grammar guides, spaced-repetition apps, and graded readers, all worked through on your own schedule. It costs the least in money but the most in willpower, because nobody checks your homework and nobody corrects your pronunciation unless you record yourself and compare it to a native speaker.

Online courses sit between self-study and a classroom. Some are pre-recorded video libraries you watch whenever you like, closer to self-study with better production values. Others include live group lessons or one-on-one sessions with a tutor over video call, which gives you real feedback and a fixed time slot that forces you to show up.

In-person language schools, often found in Tokyo, Osaka, or other cities with large expatriate populations, run daily or weekly classes in small groups. They are built for immersion: you are physically present, surrounded by other learners and often by Japanese staff, and the pace is set by the school rather than by you. Many of these schools also handle student visa sponsorship for people enrolling from abroad.

University language departments teach Japanese as part of a degree or as a standalone certificate program, usually over a full academic year or more. The pace is slower than an intensive language school but the material goes deeper, covering reading, writing, and sometimes classical grammar or literature alongside conversation.

Matching the method to your goal

If your goal is to order food, read station signs, and handle basic small talk before a trip, self-study with a phrasebook-style app and a few months of casual practice is enough. You do not need a structured course to reach that level, and paying for one would be solving a problem you don’t have.

If your goal is passing a proficiency test like the JLPT at an intermediate level, a hybrid of self-study grammar references and an online course with practice exams tends to work well. The self-study portion covers vocabulary memorization, which is repetitive and doesn’t need a teacher. The online portion gives you someone to check your written answers and explain the grammar points that textbooks explain badly.

If your goal is working in Japan or attending a Japanese university, an in-person language school or a university program is close to mandatory. These paths move faster because class time is dense and unavoidable, and many include the paperwork support that independent study cannot replicate. A reader relocating for a job that requires conversational business Japanese within a year should expect to need daily classroom hours, not an evening app habit.

If your goal is conversation fluency for family reasons, such as a Japanese-speaking partner or in-laws, live online tutoring sessions focused on speaking, rather than grammar drills, usually produce faster results than a structured course built around a textbook sequence.

Situations that change the calculation

Consider someone with a full-time job who can only study in the evenings and on weekends. A classroom school with fixed daytime hours is impractical for this person, no matter how good the teaching is, because they will miss half the sessions. An online course with recorded lessons plus a weekly live tutoring call fits that schedule better.

Consider a university student who already has free time during the semester and wants academic credit toward a degree. A university Japanese course is the obvious fit here, since the credit requirement rules out self-study and online options entirely, regardless of how good those alternatives might be for someone else.

Consider someone who already lived in Japan briefly, picked up listening comprehension, but never learned to read beyond basic kana. This person doesn’t need beginner conversation classes. They need targeted kanji and reading instruction, which points toward self-study reading materials or a tutor who can design a custom lesson plan, rather than a generic course that starts from the alphabet.

Consider a retiree planning an extended stay in Japan with no work or exam deadline. Time pressure is low, so self-study paced over a year or two, supplemented by occasional online conversation practice, covers the goal without the cost or scheduling demands of a school.

What to check before committing

Look at how much speaking time each format actually gives you. A course heavy on video lectures might involve very little active conversation, even if it calls itself comprehensive. Ask or look for a sample class before enrolling.

Check whether an in-person school or university program requires a placement test, since skipping levels can waste both time and money if you land in a class pitched below your ability.

For anyone planning to study in Japan on a student visa through a language school, confirm the visa sponsorship process directly with the school rather than assuming it, since not every school handles every nationality or every visa category the same way.

Finally, treat the four options as a progression rather than a single lifetime choice. Many learners start with self-study to see if they enjoy the language, move to an online course once they hit a plateau that needs a teacher’s feedback, and only commit to a full-time school or university program once they have a specific goal, like a job offer or an academic requirement, that justifies the heavier schedule.
