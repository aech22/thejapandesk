How to Learn Japanese Jiu Jitsu as a Complete Beginner

If you want the grappling sport with belts, gi, and ground fighting that dominates gyms worldwide, you are looking for Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ), and Japan has plenty of gyms teaching it, many with English-speaking instructors in Tokyo and Osaka. If you want the older, stand-up art of joint locks, throws, and weapon disarms that predates judo, you are looking for traditional Japanese jujutsu, and that is a smaller, more insular world you usually enter through a personal introduction rather than a walk-in trial class. Most people searching for “Japanese jiu jitsu” today actually mean the first one, so start by figuring out which art you actually want.

What is the difference between jujutsu and Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu?

Jujutsu developed over centuries among samurai retainers as a way to deal with an armed or armored opponent without a weapon of your own: throws, pins, strikes to vulnerable points, and joint locks designed to disable rather than to score points. In the late 1800s, Jigoro Kano took techniques from several jujutsu schools and reorganized them into judo, stripping out the most dangerous strikes and locks so practitioners could spar safely and often. One of Kano’s students, Mitsuyo Maeda, traveled to Brazil in the early twentieth century and taught judo and jujutsu to the Gracie family, who adapted it over decades into what is now called Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, with a much heavier emphasis on ground control and submissions than either judo or classical jujutsu.

So the lineage runs jujutsu to judo to Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, and each generation kept some pieces and dropped others. Classical jujutsu still exists in Japan today, taught in small schools that often trace their style back to a specific historical lineage, sometimes called a koryu. Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, meanwhile, has come back to Japan as its own import, taught in commercial gyms that look much like BJJ gyms anywhere else in the world.

If you train BJJ, expect most of a class to happen on the mat, on your back or on top of a partner, working toward chokes and joint locks from position. If you train classical jujutsu, expect more time standing, working through prearranged sequences with a partner who attacks in a set way so you can practice the correct response, closer to a structured drill than live sparring.

Which one should a beginner actually choose?

Choose Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu if your goal is fitness, competition, or a practical grappling skill you can test against a resisting partner from day one. The culture is used to total beginners, classes are structured around teaching white belts the basics, and most gyms let you try a session before committing.

Choose traditional jujutsu if your interest is historical, if you want to understand the techniques judo and BJJ grew out of, or if you are drawn to the discipline and formality of a classical martial art rather than a sport. Be aware that this path is slower to get into. Many koryu schools do not advertise, do not run drop-in classes, and expect a prospective student to be introduced by an existing member or to observe several sessions before being allowed to join.

If you are not sure which you want, a first BJJ class will tell you more about whether grappling interests you at all, since it is far easier to arrange on short notice.

How does a beginner start training?

For BJJ, search for gyms in the city where you will be staying or living. In Tokyo and Osaka especially, a number of gyms run classes partly or fully in English and are used to visitors passing through for a few weeks as well as long-term residents. Most will let you attend a single trial class, often free or at a reduced rate, before asking you to sign up for a month or a set number of classes. You do not need your own gi for a first visit; many gyms have loaner gear, though you will want your own rashguard and shorts for hygiene once you keep coming back.

For classical jujutsu, start by identifying the specific school or style you are interested in, since “jujutsu” covers dozens of distinct lineages with different curricula. Some larger, better-known schools do accept foreign students and have affiliated dojos outside Japan where you can start training before ever visiting. Others are small and local, attached to a single teacher, and genuinely require a personal connection. Reaching out well ahead of a trip, explaining your experience level and your interest, and asking about visiting as an observer is a more realistic approach than expecting to walk in.

What should a first class actually look like?

In a BJJ class, expect a warm-up, a technique taught in pairs with the instructor demonstrating and correcting, and then live sparring, called rolling, at the end for anyone past the absolute first few sessions. Bowing onto and off the mat, addressing the instructor as sensei, and keeping fingernails and toenails trimmed are standard etiquette almost everywhere, Japan included.

In a classical jujutsu class, expect more formality: a bow at the start that may include a short seated ritual, techniques practiced in a fixed partner pattern called kata, and far less free sparring, if any. Students are often corrected on small details of posture and distance that look minor to an outsider but matter a great deal to the instructor. Patience with repetition is part of the training itself.

What should a beginner check before signing up?

Confirm whether a gym or dojo offers a genuine trial, since policies vary and some require a membership commitment from the first visit. Ask whether classes are taught in English or whether you will need at least basic Japanese to follow instruction, particularly outside the largest cities. Check what gear is required and whether it is available to rent or buy on site, since gi sizing in Japan can run smaller than gi made for Western body types. If you are only in Japan briefly, mention that upfront, since some gyms have short-term passes built for travelers while others are structured around monthly members only.

Finally, if your real interest is the deeper history behind the techniques rather than a workout, read about the specific koryu school before you travel, since each one teaches a different slice of a much older tradition, and showing up with some sense of that history tends to get a warmer welcome than showing up without it.
