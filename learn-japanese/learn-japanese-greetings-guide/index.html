Essential Japanese Greetings for Everyday Situations

Learn five or six greetings tied to specific moments of the day and you can get through most ordinary encounters in Japan: a morning hello to a shop clerk, a first-time introduction, a thank-you after a meal, a goodbye at a train station. The trick is not memorizing a long list but matching the phrase to the moment and knowing whether the people around you expect something polite or something relaxed.

What greeting fits the time of day

Japanese greetings are tied to clock time more strictly than English ones. “Ohayou gozaimasu” is the morning greeting, used from waking up until roughly late morning. Drop the “gozaimasu” and say just “ohayou” with friends, family, or coworkers you’re close to, and keep the full version for anyone older, a boss, or a stranger.

From midday onward, “konnichiwa” takes over. It works for most of the afternoon and has no casual shortcut the way the morning greeting does — it’s used the same way with strangers and friends alike, though tone and a nod or bow change depending on who you’re talking to.

Once evening sets in, switch to “konbanwa.” This one is a standalone greeting, not something you’d say when leaving for the night — that’s a different phrase entirely (covered below). Think of “konbanwa” as the evening equivalent of “konnichiwa”: a hello, not a goodbye.

A small practical note: these three greetings overlap at the edges. Nobody switches from “ohayou gozaimasu” to “konnichiwa” at a fixed minute. If unsure, listen to what the person you’re greeting says first and mirror it.

How to greet someone you’re meeting for the first time

First meetings call for “hajimemashite,” which roughly opens the introduction, followed by your name and then “yoroshiku onegaishimasu.” That last phrase doesn’t translate neatly into English — it’s closer to “please treat me well going forward” or “I look forward to working with you” — and it shows up constantly in business, school, and even casual introductions between new neighbors.

A slight bow usually accompanies this exchange, deeper and held longer in business contexts, brief and casual among younger people meeting at, say, a shared hobby group. If someone hands you a business card, it’s worth receiving it with both hands and taking a moment to look at it rather than pocketing it right away — the greeting isn’t complete until that small gesture happens.

If the meeting is informal — meeting a friend’s friend at a bar, for instance — people often skip straight to “yoroshiku” without the full “hajimemashite,” and that shortened version is completely normal among people roughly the same age.

Saying goodbye depends on who’s leaving and for how long

“Sayounara” is the goodbye most learners pick up first, but it carries a slight air of finality — it suggests you won’t see the person again for a while, sometimes a long while. Classmates don’t usually say it to each other every afternoon; it shows up more in formal farewells, graduation speeches, or scenes in dramas where someone is leaving for good.

For everyday partings, Japanese speakers lean on more specific phrases. Leaving work before your colleagues calls for “osaki ni shitsurei shimasu” — a polite acknowledgment that you’re stepping out first. The colleagues staying behind might respond with “otsukaresama desu,” a phrase that doesn’t map onto any single English expression but functions as a recognition of the other person’s effort that day; it gets used at the end of meetings, after a shift, even in text messages that close out a work exchange.

Among friends, “jaa ne,” “mata ne,” or “bai bai” (a borrowed version of “bye-bye”) cover casual goodbyes, implying you expect to see the person again soon. Family members leaving the house in the morning often say “itte kimasu” (“I’m off, and I’ll be back”), and the people staying behind answer with “itterasshai.” Coming home reverses it: “tadaima” announces your return, and whoever’s inside answers “okaeri” or the more formal “okaerinasai.”

Where polite and casual speech actually diverge

The line between keigo (polite/formal speech) and casual speech isn’t about vocabulary alone — it’s about who you’re speaking to and the social distance between you. Age, workplace rank, and familiarity all shift which version is appropriate.

Adding “gozaimasu” or “desu” to the end of a phrase is usually what moves it from casual to polite. “Ohayou” becomes “ohayou gozaimasu.” “Arigatou” becomes “arigatou gozaimasu.” Store clerks, hotel staff, and anyone in a service role will default to the polite form regardless of your age, and responding in kind — even with imperfect pronunciation — reads as respectful rather than distant.

With close friends, siblings, or people clearly younger than you, dropping the polite ending isn’t rude; it’s expected, and using the formal version with a close friend can actually sound oddly stiff, almost like you’re keeping them at arm’s length. The safer default for visitors, though, is to start polite and let the other person signal when it’s fine to relax the language — a shift to first names, more casual contractions, or a direct comment like “keigo wa iranai yo” (you don’t need formal speech).

Situations where the right greeting matters most

Walking into a small restaurant or shop, staff often greet you with “irasshaimase” — a welcome phrase you’re not expected to respond to, though a nod is fine. Ordering food or leaving a shop, “gochisousama deshita” after a meal and a simple “arigatou gozaimasu” on the way out cover almost every transaction.

At a traditional inn or when meeting a host for the first time, combining “konnichiwa” or “konbanwa” with a slight bow and then introducing yourself with “hajimemashite” works well, especially if staff have been expecting your arrival and greet you by name.

On trains or in crowded spaces, greetings matter less than small gestures — a brief nod, stepping aside, saying “sumimasen” (excuse me) rather than a formal hello. Reserve full greetings for direct, face-to-face interactions rather than passing strangers.

What to check once you’re there

Regional dialects change some of this. Parts of Kansai and other regions use local greeting variants that differ from the standard Tokyo-based forms covered here, and hearing an unfamiliar version doesn’t mean you’ve learned something wrong — just that you’re in a place with its own speech patterns. If a specific workplace, homestay family, or tour group has its own preferred greeting customs, following their lead will serve you better than sticking rigidly to a textbook phrase.
