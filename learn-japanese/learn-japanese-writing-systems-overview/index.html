Hiragana, Katakana, Kanji: How Japan’s Three Writing Systems Work Together

Learn hiragana first, katakana second, and kanji gradually after that. Hiragana and katakana are each a fixed set of about 46 basic characters, so they can be memorized within a few weeks. Kanji is open-ended, with thousands in everyday use, and gets learned in layers over years rather than finished in one pass. There is no single “Japanese alphabet” to master the way you’d learn the Roman alphabet — the language runs on three systems at once, and that’s the first thing to accept before anything else makes sense.

Why there’s no one alphabet

English speakers naturally look for the Japanese equivalent of A-B-C: one list, learn it, done. Japanese doesn’t work that way. It uses three separate scripts in the same sentence, each doing a different job. A single line of text might mix all three: hiragana for grammar endings, katakana for a borrowed word, and kanji for the core nouns and verbs. None of the three is “the alphabet” that replaces the other two. They’re not competing options either — they’re specialized tools used together, the way English text might mix regular letters, numerals, and the occasional italicized foreign word, except far more systematically.

This is also why flashcard apps that promise “learn the Japanese alphabet in a week” are misleading. What they usually mean is hiragana, or sometimes hiragana plus katakana. That’s a real and achievable goal. But it’s not the whole writing system, and treating it as the finish line leads to confusion later when kanji-heavy text shows up and nothing looks familiar.

What each script is actually for

Hiragana is the foundational script. It’s phonetic, meaning each character represents a sound rather than a meaning, and it covers every sound in the Japanese language. It’s used for grammatical elements: verb endings, particles, and connecting words. It also stands in for kanji that a child or learner hasn’t encountered yet, which is why children’s books are written almost entirely in hiragana. If you could use only one script to be understood, hiragana would get you furthest, even though actual Japanese never works that way in practice.

Katakana is also phonetic and shares the same set of sounds as hiragana, just with a different, more angular set of shapes. Its job is narrower: foreign loanwords, country and foreign personal names, onomatopoeia, and a stylistic emphasis role similar to italics in English. A word borrowed from English, for instance, gets written in katakana rather than hiragana, which signals to a Japanese reader that it’s a loanword before they’ve even parsed the sound. Menus, tech vocabulary, and brand names lean heavily on katakana for this reason.

Kanji are characters borrowed from Chinese, each carrying meaning rather than just sound. A single kanji can represent a whole concept — mountain, water, person — and most kanji have multiple possible pronunciations depending on context, which is part of why they take longer to learn than the two phonetic scripts. Kanji carry the core vocabulary of written Japanese: nouns, the stems of verbs and adjectives, and most of the information-dense content in a sentence. Newspapers, signage, and formal writing use kanji heavily; strip them out and a sentence becomes technically readable in hiragana but much harder to scan quickly, the way reading only phonetic spelling slows down an English reader used to whole-word recognition.

The order that actually works

Start with hiragana. It’s the script every beginner textbook uses to write example sentences, and it’s the one you’ll need to read furigana — the small hiragana printed above kanji in children’s materials, manga, and some signage to show pronunciation. Without hiragana, you can’t use that crutch at all.

Move to katakana once hiragana feels automatic rather than something you’re sounding out letter by letter. The two scripts represent the same sounds, so the second one goes faster once the first is solid. Katakana shows up constantly in daily life — on menus, product packaging, and station signage — so it earns its place early even though it covers a narrower range of vocabulary.

Kanji comes last, but “last” doesn’t mean waiting until hiragana and katakana are perfect. In practice, kanji study starts overlapping with the phonetic scripts fairly early, usually with the handful of simple, high-frequency characters that appear everywhere: numbers, days of the week, basic body parts. The difference is that kanji learning never really finishes the way hiragana and katakana do. Japanese schoolchildren spend nine years learning the kanji taught in compulsory education, and adult fluency assumes a few thousand characters, each with its own meaning, multiple readings, and stroke order. Learners typically keep adding kanji for years, often indefinitely, rather than closing the chapter and moving on.

What this looks like day to day

If you’re staring at a Japanese menu, the dish names are often a mix: kanji for the main ingredient, hiragana for a grammatical connector, and katakana if the dish borrows a foreign word like “gratin” or “sauce.” Recognizing which script you’re looking at tells you something before you even know the word — katakana flags a loanword you might actually recognize once sounded out, kanji flags a concept you’ll need to look up if the character is unfamiliar.

If you’re reading a train station sign, place names are usually in kanji with furigana or romanized text underneath for exactly the reason above: kanji alone assumes a vocabulary many visitors and even some residents haven’t built yet. That furigana is hiragana, which is one more reason hiragana fluency pays off early — it unlocks the pronunciation of kanji you haven’t learned yet, everywhere that furigana is provided.

If you’re trying to type in Japanese on a phone or computer, you’ll typically type in Roman letters or kana and the system converts it to the appropriate mix of hiragana, katakana, and kanji automatically, offering choices when a word could be written multiple ways. This is often how learners first get comfortable recognizing kanji they can’t yet write by hand — by picking the correct one from a list rather than drawing it from memory.

What to check before committing to a method

Textbooks and apps vary in how early they introduce kanji, and there’s no single correct pace. Some beginner courses delay kanji for months and lean on romanized text or hiragana throughout; others introduce a small set of kanji from the first lessons. Either approach can work, but it helps to know which one you’re using, since switching methods midway can feel like starting over if the underlying assumptions about script knowledge don’t match.

It’s also worth checking whether a course or app teaches both common forms readers will encounter — print kanji versus handwritten stroke order, or the small stylistic variations in how certain kana are printed in different fonts. These details rarely change the meaning, but they can throw off a learner who has only ever seen one typeface.
