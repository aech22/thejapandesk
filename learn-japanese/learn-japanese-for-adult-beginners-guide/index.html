Learn Japanese for Adult Beginners: A Practical Guide

Start with hiragana and katakana before anything else, then move into basic grammar patterns and about 100 everyday words, and only after that add kanji in small daily doses. Adults who skip this order usually quit within a few months, not because Japanese is too hard, but because they tried to do everything at once. The fix is sequencing: one skill mastered before the next begins, fitted into blocks of time that already exist in a normal workweek.

Why adults struggle more than they expect

Most adult beginners assume the hard part will be grammar. In practice, the first wall is the writing system. English speakers are used to reading instantly, and the two or three weeks it takes to get hiragana and katakana into long-term memory feels disproportionately slow. The second wall is time. A student with a job, a commute, and a family has maybe thirty to forty minutes a day for study, split into fragments rather than one long session. The third wall is comparison. Adults compare their halting sentences to native speech they hear in dramas or at work, and the gap feels discouraging in a way it never did when they were children learning their first language with no audience watching.

None of these walls are unusual. They are the normal shape of adult language learning, and the sequence below is built around all three.

The order that actually works

First, hiragana and katakana, learned through writing by hand rather than just recognizing them on a screen. This typically takes two to three weeks of short daily sessions. Trying to read real sentences before this step is finished only slows things down, because every few seconds of reading turns into decoding instead of comprehension.

Second, a beginner grammar course that introduces sentence patterns in a fixed order, such as the Genki or Minna no Nihongo textbook series, or an equivalent structured app. The point of a structured course over random phrase apps is that each lesson depends on the last one, so skipping around creates gaps that surface later as confusion about particles like wa, ga, and wo.

Third, kanji, introduced gradually and tied to vocabulary you are already using rather than studied as an isolated list. Most working adults do better learning five to ten kanji a week attached to words from their current lessons than trying to memorize two thousand characters from a frequency list with no context.

Fourth, listening and speaking practice, started earlier than most self-taught learners expect. Waiting until grammar feels “ready” before speaking is one of the most common reasons adults stay stuck at a beginner level for years. Speaking badly from month two, even in short exchanges with a tutor or language partner, builds the reflexes that textbook study alone does not.

Fitting study around a job

The adults who keep going past the first six months are usually the ones who attach study to an existing habit rather than carving out a new block of free time. A commute becomes a listening slot. A lunch break becomes ten minutes of flashcard review. An evening that already includes watching something on a screen becomes an evening with Japanese subtitles instead of English ones.

Consistency matters more than session length. Fifteen minutes every day moves a beginner further in six months than two hours once a week, because language retention depends on spaced repetition, and spacing only works if the gaps between study sessions stay short and regular.

If a work project consumes a week entirely, the goal shifts from learning something new to not losing what is already there. A single flashcard review session or one short video with subtitles keeps the material from decaying, even when nothing new gets added.

What this looks like week to week

Consider a reader who wants to speak Japanese casually with coworkers or in-laws within a year, studying alongside a full-time job. Weeks one through three go entirely to hiragana and katakana, using handwriting practice sheets or an app that requires recall rather than recognition. Weeks four through twelve work through the first third of a beginner textbook, with short listening exercises added once basic sentence patterns are in place. From month four onward, kanji practice runs alongside the textbook, five to ten characters a week, always attached to vocabulary already in use. By month six, a weekly session with an online tutor or a language exchange partner turns passive knowledge into something usable in real conversation.

Another reader with a less predictable schedule, such as shift work or frequent travel for a job, might stretch the same sequence over twice the time. The order stays the same even when the pace changes, because each stage depends on the one before it.

Caveats and what to check for yourself

Textbook series go through editions, and course content at any given school changes from term to term, so confirm the current edition and syllabus directly with the publisher or school before buying materials. Language apps update their feature sets often enough that a method described here as effective today may look different in a year; check current reviews before committing to a paid subscription.

If conversation practice matters most to a reader’s goals, a tutor or exchange partner should be added earlier in the sequence than described above, even before kanji study begins in earnest. The order in this guide optimizes for steady, sustainable progress for someone balancing study with a full-time job, not for the fastest possible route to fluency. Adjust the pace, not the sequence, to match how much time a given week actually allows.
