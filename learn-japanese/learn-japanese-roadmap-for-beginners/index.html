Learn Japanese Basics: The Order That Actually Works

Start with hiragana, then katakana, then move into basic grammar and vocabulary together, and let kanji grow slowly alongside that grammar rather than as a separate project. Save heavy conversation practice for once you can read simple sentences and string together basic phrases — trying to speak before you have any reading foundation usually means relearning everything twice. This order isn’t the only one that works, but it’s the one that creates the fewest dead ends for someone starting from zero.

Why the order matters more than the method

Japanese has three writing systems and a grammar structure that doesn’t map onto English. Beginners who skip around — a little kanji here, a conversation app there, a grammar book they abandon after chapter two — tend to plateau fast. The problem usually isn’t motivation. It’s that each skill in Japanese depends on the one before it. You can’t read example sentences in a grammar textbook if you don’t know hiragana. You can’t guess the pronunciation of a new kanji if you don’t already know common vocabulary. Building in sequence means each new thing you learn reinforces what came before it, instead of sitting in isolation.

Step one: hiragana

Hiragana is the first writing system because nearly every beginner resource assumes you already know it. It’s a closed set of 46 base characters, so it’s learnable in a short, defined burst rather than an open-ended task. Most people get functional recognition within one to two weeks of daily practice using writing repetition, flashcards, or both.

Don’t wait until you have hiragana memorized perfectly before moving on. Partial fluency — being able to sound out words slowly — is enough to start step two. Perfection comes from use, not from drilling the chart in isolation for a month.

Step two: katakana

Katakana represents foreign loanwords, names, and some onomatopoeia. It’s visually different enough from hiragana that studying them back-to-back without a gap causes confusion for a lot of learners — some characters look like mirror images of their hiragana counterparts, and it’s easy to mix them up.

A practical approach is to get hiragana to a comfortable level first, then spend a focused week or two on katakana using words you already recognize, like coffee or taxi, borrowed from English. Katakana usually goes faster than hiragana because the words themselves are already familiar.

Step three: grammar and vocabulary together

Once both kana systems are usable, grammar and vocabulary should be learned side by side, not vocabulary first and grammar later. Japanese sentence structure — verb at the end, particles marking the role of each word, no articles like “a” or “the” — is different enough from English that vocabulary without grammar is nearly useless. Knowing the words for “eat,” “I,” and “sushi” doesn’t tell you how to say “I eat sushi” if you don’t know where the particles go.

This is the stage to pick a structured course or textbook rather than relying only on apps built around isolated vocabulary lists. A textbook introduces grammar points in a deliberate sequence and supplies vocabulary that matches each point, which keeps the two skills moving together instead of drifting apart.

Step four: kanji, introduced gradually

Kanji is the piece that intimidates most beginners, and the mistake is treating it as a wall to climb before anything else. It works better as a steady background task that starts once you have basic grammar underway, not before and not as a standalone sprint.

Learn kanji attached to vocabulary you’re already using. If you’ve learned the word for “water” in hiragana through your grammar study, learning its kanji form next reinforces a word you already recognize, rather than asking you to memorize an abstract character with no context. Many learners pace this by frequency — starting with the few hundred kanji that appear most often in everyday writing — rather than trying to memorize characters in the order a dictionary lists them.

Kanji study never really finishes. Even advanced learners keep adding to their recognition over years. The goal early on isn’t completeness; it’s building enough recognition that reading doesn’t feel like decoding a puzzle every single sentence.

Step five: conversation practice

Speaking comes last in this sequence, but not because it’s unimportant — it’s because speaking fluently draws on everything above it. Once you have usable kana, a working grammar foundation, and enough vocabulary to form sentences, conversation practice is what turns textbook knowledge into something that functions in real time.

This is also the stage where a language exchange partner, a tutor, or a conversation-focused class pays off. Practicing speaking too early, before you have grammar structure, tends to produce fluent-sounding but incorrect habits that are harder to unlearn later than they would have been to avoid in the first place.

If you’re starting with almost no time

Someone with fifteen minutes a day should still follow the same order, just stretched out. Hiragana first, even in small daily doses, because every other step depends on it. Skipping straight to conversational phrases without kana might feel productive, but it tends to create a ceiling fast, because you’ll have no way to study materials beyond whatever phrase list you started with.

If you already know some kanji from another source

Some learners arrive having picked up a handful of kanji from anime subtitles, work, or visits to Japan, without having studied hiragana properly. In that case, it’s worth backfilling hiragana and katakana before going further, even if it feels like a step backward. Reading comprehension built on guessed kanji without a kana foundation tends to be shakier than it looks, and it will show up later in listening and writing.

What to check before committing to a course or app

Not every textbook or app follows this sequence. Some apps teach isolated vocabulary without grammar context, which works for travel phrases but builds a weaker foundation for actual fluency. Before committing time to a resource, check whether it introduces hiragana and katakana explicitly at the start, whether it pairs new vocabulary with grammar points rather than presenting word lists alone, and whether it has a plan for introducing kanji gradually rather than all at once partway through the course. A resource that handles all three is usually worth the investment of sticking with it past the first few weeks.
