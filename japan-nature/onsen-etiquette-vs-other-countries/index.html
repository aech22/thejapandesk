Hot Spring Etiquette: How Japan Differs From Korea, China, and Iceland

Japan’s onsen culture is stricter about nudity and tattoos than Korea’s jjimjilbang, more insistent on washing before entering the water than many Chinese hot spring resorts, and far less casual about swimwear than Iceland’s geothermal pools. If you have soaked in a Korean sauna or floated in an Icelandic lagoon and assume the same rules apply in Japan, you will be corrected, sometimes politely and sometimes not. The core difference is this: Japanese onsen treat the bath itself as a shared, swimsuit-free space governed by a specific pre-bath ritual, while the other three cultures build more flexibility into how people enter the water.

Why Japan’s Rules Feel So Fixed

A Japanese onsen is built around one idea: the water you soak in must stay clean for everyone else. That means washing thoroughly at the shower stations before stepping into the tub, never letting soap or shampoo touch the bath water, and never wearing a swimsuit in the communal bath itself. Small hand towels are for modesty while walking between the shower area and the tub, not for scrubbing in the water, and they come off before you get in. Long hair gets tied up so it doesn’t trail in the bath. Tattoos are still a sticking point at many traditional establishments, where they’ve historically been read as a signal of organized crime affiliation, though attitudes are loosening at hotel and resort baths that cater to overseas guests.

The gender separation is also strict and fixed for the duration of a visit. Men’s and women’s baths are divided by curtain color (red or pink for women, blue for men, as a near-universal but not absolute convention) and visitors stay on their assigned side. Mixed-gender bathing, called konyoku, still exists in a handful of rural, often rotenburo (outdoor) settings, but it is no longer the default anywhere, and most visitors will never encounter it.

Where Korea’s Jjimjilbang Culture Diverges

Korean bathhouses, or jjimjilbang, share the nudity-in-the-bath-itself rule with Japan, so the shock of a swimsuit-free tub room isn’t new to someone coming from Seoul. What’s different is everything built around the bath. A Korean jjimjilbang is usually one part of a much larger complex that includes a common lounge area where men and women mix freely, wearing the matching shorts and t-shirt the facility issues at the entrance. Visitors nap on heated floors, watch television, and eat snacks in that shared space, something that has no real equivalent at a Japanese onsen, where the bath is the destination and little else happens on-site beyond a changing room and maybe a rest lounge.

Korean baths are also generally more tolerant of tattoos, and scrubbing services, where an attendant exfoliates your skin while you lie on a table, are a normal add-on that simply doesn’t exist in the Japanese bathing script. A traveler used to booking a scrub at a Korean spa may look for the same option at a ryokan and not find it, because the onsen experience in Japan centers on the water and the heat, not on additional treatments happening poolside.

China’s Hot Spring Resorts Are More Relaxed About Coverage

Many hot spring resorts in mainland China, particularly larger resort-style properties, allow or even expect swimwear in the main pools, especially in mixed-gender outdoor areas with multiple themed pools. This is the opposite of the Japanese default, and it’s the single most common point of confusion for visitors moving between the two countries in one trip. Someone who packed a swimsuit for a week that includes both a Chinese hot spring resort and a Japanese onsen town needs to know the suit stays in the bag once they cross into Japan’s indoor bathing areas, because wearing it there signals that you haven’t understood the etiquette, not that you’re being appropriately modest.

Pre-bath washing is also treated with less formality at many Chinese hot spring resorts, where a quick rinse under a shower might substitute for the seated, soap-and-rinse routine expected before entering a Japanese tub. Visitors arriving in Japan straight from this kind of resort culture sometimes skip the washing stations entirely, which is the fastest way to draw a correction from onsen staff or other bathers.

Iceland’s Geothermal Pools Run on an Entirely Different Logic

Iceland sits furthest from Japan’s customs. Swimsuits are mandatory in geothermal lagoons and public pools, and nudity in the communal bathing areas themselves would be out of place. But Iceland shares one surprising overlap with Japan: the pre-bath shower. Icelandic pools require a thorough, soap-and-water shower before entering, and at many municipal pools this is enforced with signage, attendants, or both. The difference is that Icelanders shower nude in communal shower rooms before putting on their swimsuit to enter the pool, a sequence that can feel backward to a visitor expecting either full nudity throughout (as in Japan) or full coverage throughout (as they might assume from the swimsuit requirement).

For someone arriving in Japan after a trip to the Blue Lagoon or a local Icelandic pool, the adjustment runs in the opposite direction from the China comparison: the swimsuit that was required in Iceland needs to disappear once you reach the Japanese bath itself, even though the washing habit from Iceland transfers over almost perfectly.

Situations That Catch Visitors Off Guard

A traveler with visible tattoos researching options in Japan should check specific policies before arriving rather than assuming a Korean or Icelandic-style tolerance will apply; some facilities offer cover-up stickers or private baths as a workaround, others simply decline entry. Anyone used to the Korean habit of lounging in shared common areas between bath sessions should know that most Japanese onsen don’t have that mixed social space at all, so the etiquette adjustment is really about expectation, not behavior.

Families coming from a Chinese resort where children swim in shorts should check in advance whether a specific onsen allows children in bathing attire at all age ranges, since policies vary by facility and by the age of the child. And visitors coming from Iceland who are used to showering nude before dressing for the pool should simply skip the dressing step once they reach the tub room in Japan, since the shower habit is correct but the swimsuit habit is not.

What to Check Before You Go

Rules differ enough between individual establishments, especially around tattoos, private bath options, and mixed bathing, that it’s worth checking a specific inn or bathhouse’s posted policy rather than assuming a blanket national rule. Local tourism offices and ryokan staff will usually answer directly if asked, and many hot spring towns post multilingual etiquette guides near the changing rooms for exactly this reason.
