Onsen Etiquette at Hotels and Ryokan: What Changes From Public Baths

At a hotel or ryokan, the onsen is included in your stay, open for set hours rather than a single paid visit, and used by a small, known group of guests rather than a changing crowd off the street. That changes how you pace yourself, what you carry back and forth, and how much you need to watch the clock. The core rules — wash before soaking, no swimsuits, tattoos may need covering or checking in advance — stay the same everywhere. What shifts is the rhythm around them.

Why the setting changes the manners

A public bathhouse, the kind found in a hot spring town, runs on turnover. Someone pays at the door, bathes, and leaves to make room for the next person. Staff or a sign at the entrance usually post rules for a reason: the room fills and empties all day, and nobody there has seen you before. Kinosaki’s public baths work this way, charging ¥800 per adult visit (¥400 for children aged 3 to primary school), with a day pass at ¥1,500 for adults and ¥750 for children for guests who want to try more than one bathhouse in the same evening. Kurokawa Onsen uses a similar idea with its nyuto-tegata, a pass priced at ¥1,500 for adults and ¥700 for children that carries three stickers so a guest can visit three different inn baths across the town, usable between 8:30 and 21:00 and valid for six months from purchase. Either way, you are a guest for the length of one soak, and the etiquette reflects that: move efficiently, don’t monopolize a tub, leave the changing room as you found it.

A hotel or ryokan bath is different because you are not passing through. You’re staying the night, sometimes several nights, and the bath is one amenity among several rather than the whole point of the stop. Staff know the guest list. The room is usually open across a long window in the evening and again in the morning rather than a single transaction. That gives you more room to linger, but it also means the small courtesies toward other guests matter more, since you may see the same people again at breakfast.

What actually changes step by step

The washing stage is identical everywhere: sit at a washing station, rinse and soap yourself fully, rinse again, and only then enter the tub. No exceptions, no shortcuts, regardless of whether you’re at a roadside bathhouse or a hotel on the twentieth floor.

The towel changes slightly. At a public bath you often bring your own small towel or rent one at the counter, and you carry it in with you, keeping it out of the water by folding it on your head or setting it at the tub’s edge. At a hotel or ryokan, a small towel is usually provided in your room along with a yukata, and the routine is the same once you’re in the bathing area, but you don’t need to think about renting or buying one, and a larger towel for drying off is waiting back in your room rather than at a vending machine.

Timing is the biggest difference. A public bathhouse has posted hours that apply to everyone equally, and once it closes, it closes. A ryokan bath often has separate hours for men and women, sometimes with a swap partway through the evening so both groups get a turn at a rotenburo or a bath with a better view. Some inns post one combined schedule for the whole stay; others change the gender assignment at a fixed hour each night. Check the sign by the bath entrance or ask at the front desk when you check in, since getting this wrong means walking in on the wrong group.

Tattoo policy also tends to be more flexible at a private establishment than at a public one. Many ryokan will allow small tattoos if covered with a patch, or will offer a private bath (kashikiri-buro) booking for guests who can’t or don’t want to cover up. A municipal bathhouse is less likely to make exceptions, since staff don’t know each guest and the rule has to apply uniformly. If a tattoo is a concern, it’s worth asking the ryokan directly when booking rather than discovering the policy at the door.

Situations that trip people up

If you arrive late in the evening after a long travel day, a hotel or ryokan bath is usually still your best option, since the hours typically stretch past what a public facility would offer, and your luggage can stay in your room rather than needing to be managed at a public locker.

If you’re staying at a ryokan with a shared bath and want privacy, ask about a private bath slot rather than assuming the main bath is first-come, first-served in a way that guarantees you space alone. Some inns take reservations for a small private tub by the hour; others leave it open and guests simply wait their turn.

If you’re visiting a hot spring town and want to try more than one bath, that’s when a town-wide pass like Kurokawa’s nyuto-tegata or Kinosaki’s Yumepa makes more sense than paying at each door, since you’re moving between separate buildings rather than staying inside one property.

If you’re traveling with a small child, check whether the hotel bath allows children in the adult area at all, and from what age. Some ryokan restrict older children from the opposite-gender parent’s bath past a certain age, usually around early elementary school, while younger children can go in with either parent.

What to check before you go in

Look for a sign at the bath entrance listing hours and, if applicable, which gender uses which bath at what time. Ask at check-in if nothing is posted in English. If you have a tattoo, raise it when you book rather than at the bath door. And if the ryokan’s bath is small or heavily used at peak hours around dinner and breakfast, going at an off hour, late at night or early morning, usually means more space and less waiting, the same logic that applies at any public bathhouse but with a longer window to work with.
