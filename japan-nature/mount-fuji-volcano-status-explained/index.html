Why Mount Fuji Is Classified as an Active Volcano

Mount Fuji is an active volcano, not a dormant or extinct one, and Japan’s government treats it that way: it sits under continuous monitoring by the Japan Meteorological Agency, and both the prefectures around it and the national government maintain hazard maps and evacuation plans built around the possibility of another eruption. For a climber, this does not mean avoiding the mountain. It means checking current alert status before a trip, understanding what the designation is based on, and knowing that the quiet, symmetrical cone seen from the Shinkansen is a geological feature with a documented history of eruption, not a sleeping statue.

What makes a volcano “active” in the first place

Volcanologists in Japan define an active volcano as one that has erupted within roughly the last 10,000 years, or one that currently shows signs of volcanic activity such as fumaroles, gas emission, or measurable ground deformation. By that standard, a very long quiet period does not change the classification. Mount Fuji’s most recent eruption was in 1707, known as the Hoei eruption, which threw ash as far as modern-day Tokyo and left a visible secondary crater, Hoei-zan, on the mountain’s southeastern flank. More than three centuries without an eruption sounds like a long time on a human scale, but on a geological scale it is a short pause. Japan sits atop several tectonic plate boundaries, and Mount Fuji formed from the interaction of at least three of them, which is part of why the country has dozens of volcanoes under similar watch.

The active designation is not a prediction. It is a statement about mechanism: the magma chamber beneath the mountain is still considered capable of producing an eruption, and the monitoring agencies have not found reason to reclassify it as extinct. Extinct volcanoes are ones geologists believe have permanently lost their magma supply. Mount Fuji has not reached that status, and there is no scientific consensus that it ever will in any timeframe relevant to a reader planning a trip.

How the mountain is actually watched

The Japan Meteorological Agency runs a network of seismometers, tiltmeters, and GPS stations around Mount Fuji that track small earthquakes, shifts in the ground surface, and gas composition. This is the same kind of network used on other closely watched Japanese volcanoes, and it operates continuously, not just during climbing season. The agency assigns volcanic alert levels, similar in concept to the levels used for storms or earthquakes, that move up or down based on what the instruments detect. For most of the mountain’s recent history, that level has sat at the baseline reading, meaning no signs of unusual activity.

This is the detail that matters most for a visitor. The classification “active volcano” is a long-term geological fact. The alert level is the short-term operational signal, and it is what local authorities and transit operators would act on if conditions changed. A hiker does not need to track seismic data personally, but it is worth knowing that the system exists and that closures or restrictions on the mountain would be driven by it rather than by guesswork.

What this means for someone climbing

During the official climbing season, the trails up Mount Fuji are busy, well marked, and supported by mountain huts and staff who are in regular contact with local disaster management offices. The volcanic risk does not change the practical advice that applies to any Japanese mountain: check current trail and weather conditions before setting out, follow any posted notices at trailheads, and carry a way to receive emergency information, since Japan’s area-wide alert systems can push warnings directly to mobile phones.

Some of the warning signs the monitoring network looks for, such as new fumarole activity or a cluster of shallow earthquakes, would likely trigger visible changes on the mountain itself before they reached a wider alert: trail closures, hut evacuations, or guidance from rangers. A climber who checks official notices before heading up and pays attention to any posted changes at the fifth station or along the trail is following the same precaution that applies to volcanic hazards anywhere in Japan, including Mount Aso or Sakurajima, both of which see far more frequent minor activity than Fuji does.

What it means for people living nearby

For residents of Yamanashi and Shizuoka prefectures, which share the mountain between them, the active classification has shaped decades of public planning. Hazard maps model potential ash fall, lava flow paths, and lahars, which are fast-moving flows of volcanic debris and water that can occur even without a full eruption. Schools, train operators, and expressway managers in the region have reference plans for what to do if the alert level rises, covering everything from ash cleanup logistics to temporary rail suspensions, since even a moderate ash fall can affect visibility and electrical equipment well beyond the immediate area.

This planning does not reflect a sense that an eruption is imminent. It reflects standard risk management for a known hazard, the same reasoning that leads coastal areas to maintain tsunami evacuation routes or earthquake-prone cities to reinforce buildings. Living near an active volcano in Japan is common. Roughly a tenth of the world’s active volcanoes are in Japan, and the country has built its infrastructure and emergency systems around that reality for a long time.

How to think about the risk day to day

The honest way to frame Mount Fuji’s status is this: it is capable of erupting again, the monitoring network would very likely detect warning signs before a major event, and the baseline alert level reflects the absence of those signs at any given time. None of that requires a visitor to feel anxious about climbing the mountain or visiting the Fuji Five Lakes area. It does mean skipping outdated blog posts or secondhand claims about the mountain being “overdue” for an eruption, since volcanic timing does not work on a fixed schedule, and relying instead on current notices from the Japan Meteorological Agency and local visitor centers before a trip.

Check the current alert level and any trailhead notices close to your travel dates, keep a way to receive Japanese emergency alerts while on the mountain, and treat posted trail closures as final rather than optional. Beyond that, Mount Fuji behaves, for the overwhelming majority of visits, exactly like the dormant-looking peak it appears to be from a distance.
