Onsen Etiquette for LGBTQ+ Travelers: What to Expect

Most onsen in Japan are segregated by sex rather than by gender identity, and the custom is to bathe nude. If that combination worries you, the simplest solution is to book a room with a private bath (sometimes called kashikiri or kazoku-buro) or to use a facility that rents private bathing rooms by the hour. This sidesteps the locker room question entirely and lets you soak without thinking about who else is in the water. If a private option isn’t available where you’re staying, understanding how the public baths actually work will make the experience far less stressful than it sounds from the outside.

How Japanese onsen are actually organized

Traditional onsen split bathers into two areas, usually labeled with the characters for man and woman, or marked with blue and red curtains. There is no staff check of identity documents and no enforcement beyond the honor system and social expectation. Everyone undresses in a changing room attached to the bath, stores clothes in a basket or locker, and walks into the bathing area without a towel covering them (a small hand towel is for washing and modesty while walking, not for wearing into the water).

This setup was not designed with transgender or nonbinary travelers in mind, and it shows. There is no official guidance from most onsen on which side a transgender guest should use, and staff are rarely trained to handle the question if asked directly. In practice, most transgender travelers who pass comfortably as the gender matching the bath they choose report few issues, because nobody is actually scrutinizing other bathers closely. People at an onsen are there to relax, not to inspect each other.

What this means for gay and bisexual men and women

For gay and bisexual travelers whose concern is less about which side to use and more about comfort being nude among strangers of the same sex, the main adjustment is cultural rather than personal. Japanese bathing nudity is not sexualized in local custom the way it might feel in a gym locker room back home. Staring, lingering, or any behavior read as cruising is treated as a serious breach of etiquette and will draw attention from other bathers or staff quickly. The expectation is that everyone washes at the stations provided before entering the shared tub, keeps conversation low, and does not photograph or film anything — phones generally aren’t brought into the bathing area at all.

If the idea of a shared bath still feels like too much, that is a reasonable thing to plan around rather than push through. Many hot spring towns have multiple bathhouses, and checking whether a private bath is bookable in advance removes the issue without requiring any explanation to anyone.

Changing rooms and the moments that feel most exposed

The changing room, not the bath itself, is where most travelers feel the most seen. It’s smaller, brighter, and this is where clothes come off and go back on. A few habits make this easier:

Move with purpose rather than lingering. Most Japanese bathers change quickly and don’t make conversation while undressing. Taking the same brisk approach helps you blend in rather than stand out.

Use a locker near the exit if one is free, so you’re not navigating the room twice. Keep your hand towel with you from the moment you undress — it’s a normal accessory, not something that will draw a second glance.

If you bind, wear a prosthesis, or have post-surgical scarring you’d rather not discuss, know that direct questions from strangers are uncommon in Japan generally. Curiosity is far more likely to show up as a glance than a comment. Staff are not going to intervene unless there’s a tattoo policy in play (see below) or unless another bather complains, which is rare.

Tattoos, scars, and other things that draw attention

Tattoos are a separate issue from gender or sexuality, but they come up often enough for LGBTQ+ travelers with ink to be worth mentioning. Many onsen still restrict or ban visible tattoos, a holdover from their association with organized crime. Policies vary by facility: some ban tattoos outright, some allow small ones covered with a patch, and newer or tourist-oriented baths increasingly allow them. If you have tattoos, check the specific facility’s policy before you arrive, since this is not something posted consistently and front-desk staff may not volunteer the information unless asked.

Scars from surgery, medical ports, or other visible marks are not something Japanese bathing culture has a formal policy on, and they are far less likely to cause a reaction than tattoos do. If you’re self-conscious, a trip during a quieter time of day — many onsen are emptiest in the late morning on weekdays — gives you more space and fewer eyes in the room.

Choosing a private bath when you want certainty

Ryokan with in-room baths, rental family baths at larger onsen resorts, and some day-use facilities with reservable private rooms all exist specifically for groups or individuals who want to skip the communal bath. This is a completely normal thing to book and requires no explanation of why. It’s used by families with young children, people with visible medical conditions, and plenty of travelers who simply prefer privacy, so asking about it at a front desk carries no stigma.

If you’re traveling with a partner, a private bath also means you don’t have to separate for the one part of a ryokan stay that otherwise splits couples by sex.

What to check before you go

Call ahead or check a facility’s own information (rather than general forum advice) if you have a specific concern: a tattoo policy, whether single-use private baths exist, or whether the bath has a fixed closing time that affects when it’s quietest. Rules differ enough between a small-town public bathhouse and a large resort onsen that a courtesy call saves surprises.

Outside of the bath itself, Japan’s hot spring towns are generally easy, low-friction places to travel through. The etiquette that matters most — washing before entering the tub, not bringing a towel into the water, keeping noise down — is the same etiquette every bather follows, regardless of who they are or who they’re traveling with.
