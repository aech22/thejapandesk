How Onsen Etiquette Differs Across Japan’s Famous Hot Spring Towns

If you are heading to Kinosaki, plan to walk the town in a yukata and wooden geta, visiting the public bathhouses one at a time with a pass bought at your ryokan. If you are heading to Kurokawa instead, the same yukata-and-geta picture applies, but the system runs on a sticker pass rather than a bathhouse-by-bathhouse ticket, and the baths themselves are smaller and more spread along a river gorge than lined up on a shopping street. Both towns expect the same basic bathing manners underneath the surface — wash before you soak, keep your towel out of the water, tie up long hair — but the way you move between baths, and what counts as polite in each town, is not identical.

What sotoyu actually means in each town

Sotoyu, the practice of bathing at public baths scattered around a hot spring town rather than staying inside your inn’s own tub, is the whole point of a stay in Kinosaki. The town has a cluster of public baths along its main canal, and the custom is to visit several of them over one or two nights, usually in yukata, moving from one to the next on foot. Kinosaki’s public baths cost ¥800 per adult visit (¥400 for children aged 3 to primary school), or you can buy the all-day Yumepa pass for ¥1,500 for adults and ¥750 for children, which covers unlimited entry to all the baths for that day. Most ryokan guests buy the Yumepa the moment they check in, because most people visit more than one or two baths in an evening and the math works out in the pass’s favor.

Kurokawa, in Kumamoto, runs sotoyu differently. Its baths are smaller, often outdoor, and tucked along a river rather than arranged on a single commercial street. The town sells a nyuto-tegata, a wooden pass that costs ¥1,500 for adults and ¥700 for children (age 3 to sixth grade), carries three stickers, and is valid for six months from the date of purchase. Each sticker gets you into one of the participating baths, used any time within that six-month window and usable between 8:30 and 21:00. That long validity period changes the pace of a visit: in Kinosaki the Yumepa pushes you to bathe several times in a single evening, while in Kurokawa the tegata rewards spreading your three baths across a longer stay, or even keeping the unused sticker as a souvenir if you only use two.

Moving around town in a yukata

In Kinosaki, walking between baths in your ryokan’s yukata and geta is the default, and the town is built for it. The main street is lined with the baths, shops, and footbridges over the canal, all within an easy, flat walk, so geta make sense there even for visitors unused to them. Staff at the baths expect to see guests arriving in yukata and will not blink at it.

Kurokawa’s layout works against the same habit. The baths sit along a river gorge with stone steps, slopes, and unlit stretches of road after dark, and several of the baths involve a short outdoor walk between the changing area and the bath itself. Some visitors still wear yukata into town, but geta are less practical on Kurokawa’s terrain, and inns there are more likely to suggest comfortable shoes for getting between baths after sundown, saving the yukata for strolling near the ryokan itself rather than the whole circuit.

Washing, tattoos, and the things every bath expects

Underneath the differences in pass systems and footwear, the baths themselves hold to the same core rules in both towns. Rinse off at the washing stations before stepping into the communal tub. Keep your small towel with you for modesty walking between the shower area and the bath, then set it aside — never dip it into the water. Long hair gets tied up so it does not touch the bath. Loud conversation and phones are out of place in the bathing room itself.

Tattoo policies are the one area where it pays to check ahead rather than assume. Some baths in both towns still turn away visibly tattooed guests, following a long-standing association with organized crime, while others have relaxed the rule or allow a small covering patch. This varies bath by bath rather than town by town, so if you or someone in your group has a tattoo, it is worth asking at the ryokan desk which of the local baths will admit you before you set off in your yukata with a pass already in hand.

If you are only staying one night

A single night in Kinosaki still makes sense with the Yumepa pass, since the baths sit close enough together to fit two or three visits between check-in and a late dinner. A single night in Kurokawa is a tighter fit for the tegata’s three stickers, since the baths are farther apart and the paths between them slower going after dark; using just one or two stickers and saving the rest, or returning another day within the six-month window, is a normal way to handle a short stay.

If you are arriving with luggage still in hand, Kinosaki’s station sits close to the main bathing street, so most visitors drop bags at the ryokan first and change into yukata before the first bath. Kurokawa is reached by bus rather than rail, and the walk from the bus stop into the ryokan area can include slopes, so changing into yukata only once you are settled at the inn is the more comfortable order of operations.

Getting there and what else to check locally

Kinosaki sits on the San’in line and is commonly reached from Kyoto or Osaka; travelers covering the wider Kansai region sometimes pair the trip with the JR West Kansai WIDE Area Pass at ¥12,000 for adults and ¥6,000 for children, valid for five consecutive days, or the JR West Kansai-Hiroshima Area Pass at ¥17,000 for adults and ¥8,500 for children, also valid for five consecutive days, depending on where else the itinerary runs. Kurokawa has no train station nearby and is normally reached by highway bus from Fukuoka or Kumamoto, so rail passes do not apply to that leg of the trip.

Beyond tattoos and footwear, it is worth confirming a few details locally before setting out: whether your ryokan’s yukata and geta are meant to leave the building or are for indoor use only, whether a particular outside bath is closed for maintenance that day, and whether the pass you buy actually covers every bath you intend to visit, since not every bathhouse in either town participates in the standard pass system. A quick question at the front desk when you check in clears up all three.
