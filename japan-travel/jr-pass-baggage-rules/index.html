Japan Rail Pass Baggage Rules: What You Can Bring on Trains

Most suitcases travel free and unreserved on the shinkansen. If your bag’s three dimensions add up to 160cm or less, you can carry it onto any car and store it in the overhead rack or at your feet. If it adds up to more than that, up to a limit of 250cm, you need to reserve one of the oversized luggage spaces near the back row of certain cars before you board. There is no extra charge for this reservation, but you do need to arrange it in advance, and not every train has these spaces.

This matters for Japan Rail Pass holders because the pass itself does not change any of these rules. Whether you’re riding on the Nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS, priced at ¥50,000 for seven days, ¥80,000 for fourteen days, and ¥100,000 for twenty-one days for adults through 30 September 2026 (rising to ¥53,000, ¥84,000 and ¥105,000 from 1 October 2026), or on a regional pass such as the JR TOKYO Wide Pass at ¥16,000, the baggage allowance is set by the train operator, not by the ticket you’re holding. Understanding the size rule and knowing where to put your bag saves you a scramble at the platform.

How the size limit actually works

JR measures a bag by adding its height, width, and depth together. A standard carry-on suitcase, the kind airlines allow as cabin luggage, almost always falls under the 160cm threshold and needs no special handling. A large hard-shell case packed for a two-week trip, or a box of souvenirs, can easily cross into the 160-250cm range.

If your bag falls in that oversized band, you cannot simply stand it in the aisle or wedge it into the standard overhead rack. On the Tokaido, Sanyo, and Kyushu Shinkansen lines, oversized bags need a seat reservation for one of the last-row seats in specific cars, where a dedicated space behind the seat is built to hold them. This reservation is free, but it has to be made before departure, either at a ticket counter, a reservation machine, or through the relevant booking channel. Turning up at the platform with a large bag and no reservation can mean being asked to check it elsewhere or pay a handling fee at some stations.

Bags over 250cm in total size are generally not permitted in the passenger cars at all.

Where to actually put your bag

Most seats have an overhead rack sized for a standard rolling suitcase or a backpack. It is tight enough that anything wider than an ordinary carry-on will not sit comfortably, so don’t assume a mid-size case will slide in beside someone else’s bag.

At your feet is the other common option, particularly if you have a small daypack in addition to a suitcase already stored overhead. This works fine for one bag but becomes impractical if you’re traveling with a full set of luggage for two or three people.

Between cars, near the doors, some Shinkansen sets have small shelved areas, though these are not guaranteed on every train type and shouldn’t be relied on for anything you need to reserve.

If you’ve booked the oversized luggage space, it sits directly behind the last row of seats in the relevant car. You’ll need to have made that reservation for the specific service you’re on, since the space is limited to a handful of slots per train.

Situations that actually come up

If you’re arriving into Japan and heading straight from the airport onto a shinkansen with two large check-in-style suitcases, measure them before you travel. If either one crosses the 160cm combined measurement, plan to reserve the oversized space rather than hoping there’s room in a standard rack.

If you’re on a multi-city itinerary and shipping some luggage ahead through a delivery service, common at airports and many hotels, you can travel lighter with just a daypack and skip the whole size question. This is a common approach for travelers using the Nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS across a long itinerary, since it lets you move between cities without wrestling large bags through transfers.

If you’re traveling as a family with a stroller and several bags, know that a stroller folded down usually counts as ordinary luggage if it fits the size allowance, but an unfolded one takes real floor space and works better in cars with more open area near the doors, where conductors can advise on the best spot.

If you’re catching a connecting regional train after your shinkansen leg, using a pass such as the JR EAST PASS or the JR West Kansai WIDE Area Pass, remember that local and regional trains often have far less luggage space than the shinkansen. A bag that fit easily in an oversized reservation slot on the shinkansen may need to go on your lap or between your knees on a smaller regional train.

What to check before you travel

Rules around oversized luggage reservations have changed in the past and can be adjusted again, so confirm the current size threshold and reservation process on the operator’s own information at the time of your trip, particularly if you’re carrying something unusual in shape, such as a musical instrument or sports equipment.

If your itinerary includes several different rail passes, for example combining the Nationwide JAPAN RAIL PASS with a regional option like the Hokuriku Arch Pass or the JR East-South Hokkaido Rail Pass, check the baggage guidance for each operator separately. Luggage rules are set at the train and line level, not by the pass, so what’s fine on one leg of your trip may need a reservation on another.

Finally, if you’re switching from shinkansen to a narrow-gauge local line, a bus, or a ferry as part of a wider pass itinerary, ask at the station or on board about realistic space for large bags before you commit to that connection. Staff at shinkansen stations are used to helping pass holders with oversized luggage questions, and it’s easier to sort out a reservation or a locker option before you’re standing on a crowded platform with the train already approaching.
