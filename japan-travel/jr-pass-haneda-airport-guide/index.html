Using Your Japan Rail Pass from Haneda Airport

Haneda Airport does not have a JR station, so your Japan Rail Pass does not start the moment you land. Exchange your voucher for the physical pass at the JR East travel service counter inside the airport, then ride the Tokyo Monorail to Hamamatsucho Station, where it connects to the JR Yamanote Line and the rest of the JR network. The monorail ride itself is covered by the pass because it is operated by JR East, so this is the route pass holders should default to rather than the faster-looking private line next to it.

Why Haneda needs an extra step

Narita Airport sits on a JR line, so travelers arriving there can walk straight onto a train covered by their pass. Haneda is different. The airport is served by two separate railways: the Tokyo Monorail, which belongs to JR East, and the Keikyu Airport Line, which is a private operator with no relationship to the Japan Rail Pass. Only one of these two choices is included in what you already paid for.

This matters because Keikyu is the one most airport signage and taxi drivers will point you toward, since it runs to Shinagawa and on into central Tokyo through its own through-service with the Toei Asakusa Line. It is a perfectly good way to get into the city. It simply is not free if you are holding a rail pass. The monorail is.

Where to exchange the voucher

If you bought an exchange order before arriving in Japan, look for the JR East travel service counter in the terminal building. Staff there will ask for your passport and the exchange order, and will set the start date for your pass. You do not have to activate it on arrival day. If your first day or two will be spent in Tokyo before any long-distance travel, you can choose a later start date and get more use out of the validity window once it actually begins.

Counter locations, hours, and exact procedures can change, so check the current details on JR East’s own site before you fly, particularly if you are arriving on a red-eye or a very early morning flight when staffing may be limited.

Getting from the airport to the JR network

Once the pass is in hand, or if you already hold one from a previous trip, follow signs for the Tokyo Monorail inside the terminal. The monorail line runs from the airport to Hamamatsucho Station. At Hamamatsucho, step off and transfer to the Yamanote Line or the Keihin-Tohoku Line, both of which are fully covered by the pass.

From Hamamatsucho, a short ride on the Yamanote Line reaches Tokyo Station, which is the hub for Shinkansen service heading toward Kyoto, Osaka, and points west, as well as north toward Tohoku. If your hotel is closer to Shinagawa, the Yamanote and Keihin-Tohoku lines reach that station too, and Shinagawa also has its own Shinkansen platforms.

If you are heading straight to the Shinkansen

Travelers who land at Haneda and want to reach Kyoto or Osaka the same day should plan on: monorail to Hamamatsucho, local JR line to Tokyo Station, then the Shinkansen gate. With heavy luggage, this means two train changes before you even reach the bullet train, so build in extra time rather than cutting a connection close, especially if your flight was delayed.

If your onward destination is in the Kansai region specifically, it is worth comparing a regional pass against the nationwide one. The JR West Kansai WIDE Area Pass, at ¥12,000 for adults and ¥6,000 for children for five consecutive days, or the JR West Kansai-Hiroshima Area Pass at ¥17,000 for adults and ¥8,500 for children, can work out better than a nationwide pass if you are not also making a long trip through central Japan to get there.

If you are staying in Tokyo first

Not every traveler needs to activate a nationwide pass on day one. If your itinerary starts with several days in Tokyo before any Shinkansen travel, consider using local tickets for those first days and saving the nationwide pass for when you actually leave the city. The Tokyo 1-Day Ticket, at ¥1,720 for adults and ¥860 for children, and the cheaper Tokyo Metropolitan District Pass (Tokunai Pass), at ¥870 for adults and ¥430 for children, both cover a day of travel within the city’s JR lines without touching your pass’s validity window.

If your whole trip is Tokyo and the surrounding Kanto region, the JR TOKYO Wide Pass, at ¥16,000 for adults and ¥8,000 for children for three days, may cover everything you need without the cost of the nationwide pass at all.

What the nationwide pass actually costs

For exchange orders purchased through 30 September 2026, the nationwide Ordinary pass is ¥50,000 for seven days, ¥80,000 for fourteen days, and ¥100,000 for twenty-one days for adults, with child prices at ¥25,000, ¥40,000, and ¥70,000. The Green (first class) version runs ¥70,000, ¥110,000, and ¥140,000 for adults in the same tiers. From 1 October 2026, these rise to ¥53,000, ¥84,000, and ¥105,000 for Ordinary adult passes, with Green pricing increasing in step. Confirm which pricing applies before you buy, since the date of purchase of the exchange order is what determines which figure you pay, not the date you plan to arrive.

A few things to check before you land

Double-check that your flight actually arrives at Haneda and not Narita, since the two airports require completely different first moves and are not close to each other. If you are arriving late at night, confirm that the monorail is still running before you plan on it as your only option; if service has ended for the day, you may need a taxi or an overnight option and can validate the pass the next morning instead. And if anyone in your group is tempted by the Keikyu Line because it looks faster on arrival boards, remember that choosing it means paying its fare separately rather than using a ride you have already paid for through the pass.
